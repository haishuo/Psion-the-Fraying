# The Night the World Ended

*The first of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

I keep everything. It is the one thing I have never been willing to lose, which is why I have lost nearly everything else. This is the oldest room — the first one I built, before I knew I was building anything. Everything I am starts here, so this is where I start.

I was twelve.

By then I had begun to feel things I had no words for. A pressure behind the wall of my skull, like a held breath that would not let go. The shape of what someone was about to say, arriving a half-second before they said it. I thought it was ordinary. I thought everyone walked around with the world leaning on them like that. I know now what it was. I know now that it was a light, and that lights draw things in the dark.

It came for *me.* I am careful to be exact about this, because precision is the only thing I have left to give her, and because the gentler version — that it was random, that it was bad luck, that it was no one's fault — is a mercy I am not allowed. It came for me. A latent Gifted is a better house than an ordinary child: more rooms, stronger walls, a longer lease. It wanted the house. It wanted me.

And it could not have me. Whatever I was already becoming pushed back — not on purpose; I didn't know there was anything to do on purpose — but some wall I didn't know I had held the door shut. It tried, and it could not get in. A thing like that does not simply leave. It does not shrug and move to the next town. It was already inside our home by then, in the ordinary way, the way it had learned to be: patient, pleasant, wearing a face we trusted. And when it understood that I was a locked door, it did the next thing. It found a door that would open.

Elena was eight.

She thought I was the coolest person in the world. I want that written somewhere that cannot be edited, because it is true and because no one else alive knows it. She followed me from room to room. She laughed at jokes that were not funny, because I had made them. She had a gap in her front teeth and a way of dragging my name out when she wanted something — *Maar-cusss,* two syllables and a hiss — and a song our mother used to hum that she had learned without meaning to. She was going to be someone. She was already someone.

I did not see it happen. That is the part I have gone over the most, hunting for the moment, and there is no moment. There is a *before,* when she was my sister, and there is an *after,* when something was sitting in her chair using her mouth. Between them there is nothing — no scream, no struggle, no doorway I could have stood in. It does not take you the way a wolf takes you. It takes you the way water takes a footprint. One evening the thing across the table knew the words to our mother's song and did not know why they made me cry, and I understood — all at once, far too late, the way you understand a death — that my sister was gone, and that I was the only one who could tell.

It had her face. It had her hands, and her marks climbing the kitchen doorframe, and her birthday in June. It had everything of her except *her,* and it wore all of it the way you wear a coat. To our parents she was still Elena — a little quiet lately, a little strange, growing up. Only I could see the costume. I have never stopped being the only one.

Here is the arithmetic. I do it every day, so that I never once get it wrong: it wanted me, and if it had taken me, she would have lived. She would be eighteen now. She would have had ten more years of being Elena, and sixty after that. The thing that ended my sister was not aimed at her. She was what the shot struck on its way to me.

That was the night the world ended. Not with anything loud — the world ends quietly, it turns out. It ends at a kitchen table, in a face you love, in the half-second when you realize the eyes across from you are *reading* you instead of seeing you.

I could not sleep. I lay in the dark and I was already afraid of the one thing I am still afraid of: that she would fade. That the real her — the gap in her teeth, the dragged-out *Maar-cusss,* the borrowed song — would blur into the thing wearing her, until I lost her a second time, slowly, inside my own head, the way everyone else already had. So I did the only thing I knew how to do. I closed my eyes and I called her up. Her face. *Hers,* not the costume's. I went over it in the dark like a prayer — every feature, again and again — until it held, until it would stay.

I did it again the next night. And the next.

I have done it every night for ten years. I have never missed one. It is the first room I built and it will be the last one I defend, and everything else I have become — the walls, the work, the long patient walk toward the only ending that fixes anything — is scaffolding around that one small act of keeping.

She was eight years old. She thought I was the coolest person in the world.

I am not going to let her fade.
