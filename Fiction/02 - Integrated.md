# Integrated

*The second of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

There is a thing you are supposed to do, after. When something takes your sister, you go to the people whose work it is to help. You tell them what happened and you let them carry it, because they are stronger than you and they know things you don't, and that is what they are *for.*

I was twelve. I still believed there were people whose work it was to help.

They were not hard to find — they had, in fact, already been looking for me; a light in the dark draws more than one kind of thing. I told them everything. The pressure behind my eyes, the half-second of knowing, the night the world ended. The thing at the kitchen table wearing my sister like a coat. I asked them to help me. I want to be fair to the child I was: he did not ask for revenge. He asked for his sister back. He had not yet learned that there is no *back.*

They were kind. I need that on the record, because it is the center of everything I understand about them now. They were *kind.* A woman with a soft voice and a lanyard sat with me and told me I was safe, that I was one of them, that they had been waiting for me. She said I had a *gift.* She used that exact word, and I was twelve and grieving and I let her, because someone was finally telling me that the thing that had cost me Elena meant something — that it was not only a light that had drawn a predator but a *purpose.*

Then they integrated me.

I did not understand it then. I understand it now. They taught me to brace — to build the first wall, the one that stops the bleeding of thought in both directions — and they were patient, and careful, and they held my hand through it. And while one of them held my hand, another set the construct that fastened my mind to the lattice for good. The kind woman was real. The hand was real. The leash was also real. They are very good at making the leash feel like a hand. That is not a flaw in them. It is the whole design.

I kept asking about Elena. About the thing wearing Elena. At first they answered gently — *it's been logged, it's a priority, you did the right thing telling us.* Then, as the days went and I would not stop, the answers smoothed into the shape they keep for this: *these things take time; you have to understand the scale of what we manage; it will be handled on the appropriate timeline.* I had no word for it yet. I do now. It is the sound an institution makes when it has decided your emergency is a line item. They were not lying. They fully intended to get to it. They simply meant *it,* and Elena was an *it* to no one but them, and to me she was the only thing there was.

I found out about my parents the way you find out about the weather. No one sat me down; it was mentioned in passing, a box already ticked. *A necessary measure. Newly integrated Gifted can't have civilian family who know too much — it's for their protection as much as ours. They're safer not carrying it. You'll understand when you're older.*

What they had done — what they do, as routine, with the same hands that held mine — was send Memory specialists to my mother and father and quietly take us out. Not kill us. Nothing so loud. They reached into the two people who had made me and lifted out the son named Marcus and the daughter named Elena, and smoothed the place where we had been until it lay flat. They have a word for it. They call it *Correction* — as in a thing gently set right, as in a Department of Corrections, as in an error in the record now fixed.

I did not believe it. So I found a phone I was not supposed to have, and I called the one number I have never had to keep in a vault because it is cut deeper into me than the lattice ever got. My mother answered. I said *Mom.* There was a pause — small, polite, the pause you give a stranger — and she said, *I'm sorry, sweetheart, I think you have the wrong number.*

I asked if Elena was there.

She said she didn't know anyone by that name, wished me a good night, and hung up. The kindness in her voice — the ordinary, untroubled kindness of a woman who has never lost a thing — is a sound I have kept ever since, in a room I do not open.

That was the night the arithmetic grew its second line. Elena was gone from the world, and now she was gone from the only two other people who had ever carried her, and that left exactly one. One mind in which she had ever lived. Mine. I was no longer a boy who had lost his sister. I was the last place she existed — and the people who had taken her from everyone else could reach into me whenever they liked and finish the job, without ever meaning a thing by it.

So I built. Every night I had already been going over her face in the dark — but now it was not grief, or not only grief. Now it was a wall with her inside it. I learned their bracing, and then I went past it, higher and further than they taught, because the thing they never understood about handing a grieving child a set of walls is that he will not use them the way you intended. They taught me to shield my mind so the lattice would run clean. I used it to build the one room in the world they could not enter and could not edit, and I put my sister in it, and I have held the door for ten years.

They called all of this Integration. I learned what the word means. It means *made part of.* It means there is no longer a line between you and the thing that did this to you.

And somewhere in those weeks — I will tell it properly when I come to it — I learned the other thing, the one that has organized every day since. I learned how you kill one of them. It is not a knife and it is not fire. It is exactly, precisely, to the letter, what they did to my mother. The only weapon that can end the thing wearing Elena is the same instrument they used to erase the people who made her.

What it cost me to carry that — what I had to make my peace with, and how — belongs to a later page. For now it is enough to say this: they taught me everything I would need. They only thought they were teaching me something else.
