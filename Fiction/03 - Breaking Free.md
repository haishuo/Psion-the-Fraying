# Breaking Free

*The third of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

The decision did not arrive. It accreted. People imagine there is a moment — a door slammed, a vow sworn over a grave — but there was no grave, and there was no door. Only a slow accumulation of small understandings, until one morning the sum of them was a different life.

The first understanding I have already set down: that they would never get to it. I asked one final time. I was older by then, careful, good at the work — they had made me good at the work — and I asked an officer I respected, plainly, when the thing wearing my sister would be dealt with. He looked at me with real sympathy. He told me my dedication was noted, that resources were finite, that the case remained open and would be *actioned when priority allowed.* He meant it kindly. That was the morning I understood that Elena's file would outlive me inside their system — open forever, a line that would never be reached — and that if the thing was ever to be unmade, it would be unmade by the only person for whom she was not a line.

So I made myself learn the one thing I needed, and I want to be honest about what it cost, because it is the hinge of everything. The answer to *how do you kill one of them* was never hidden from me. It was in the work they had already trained me to do. A psidolon *is* its memories; take those, and there is nothing left to cohere, and it comes apart. The kill is total erasure — iterative, patient, complete. I learned it the way you learn anything in that place: thoroughly, and from people who were kind to me while they taught it.

And it is, to the letter, what they did to my mother.

I had wondered, sometimes, what I would have to become to do this. Now I knew. I would have to become, for the length of one act, exactly the thing that erased my family. The instrument is the same. There is no other instrument. I sat with that a long time. I did not weep; I had stopped, by then, being a person who wept. I did the arithmetic until it was clean: the weapon that destroyed my family is the only weapon that can end the thing that destroyed it first. So be it. I made my peace. It is not the kind of peace that lets you sleep. But I made it — and once it was made, there was only the matter of leaving.

I planned it for a long time. I am a careful person; carefulness is most of what I have. I learned the patterns, the gaps in the watch, the routes the lattice does not light. And I understood the central problem, which was my own head. I was on the network. They could read me, reach me, and — if they decided a trainee had acquired the wrong fixation — quiet me the way they quieted my parents, gently, the box already ticked. I could not hunt the thing from inside a mind they owned. To hunt it I had to be invisible; to be invisible I had to come off the lattice; and the only safe way off the lattice is a Maintainer's hands — and a Maintainer means staying.

There are others who will cut a runaway loose. There is always someone in the business of doors. But every door opens into another house, and I had decided I was done living in houses. Whatever this was, I would do it with my own hands.

So it would not be safe. There was no version that was safe. The construct that fastens you to the network is set deep, woven through the architecture of you; taking it out yourself is not unlike surgery on your own brain, with no light and no second pair of hands. People die doing it. People come out of it damaged — fragmented, less than they were, the exact ruin the whole institution exists to prevent. I knew the odds. I accepted them, because the alternative was a long safe life as a node in a machine that had eaten my family and would never, ever get to it.

The night before, I did what I do. I went into the one room they could not enter and I sat with my sister's face until it held. I told her — I am not embarrassed by this — that if the severance killed me, this would be the last thing I ever did, and that I could think of no better one. Then I checked the door of that room twice, because whatever happened to the rest of me, that was the part I could not afford to lose.

I will not set down the severance in detail. Some things you keep in a room you do not open, and that is one of them. I will say only this: I did it alone, in the dark, and it was the worst pain I have known or expect to know, and there was a long moment in the middle where I was certain it had killed me and was almost glad. And then it was done. The lattice went out of me the way a sound you have heard your whole life goes silent — you never know how loud it was until it stops. Ten years of a low hum, of a thousand other minds present at the edge of my own, of being *findable.* Gone. The silence was enormous. I had never, in my remembered life, been alone before. Not really. Not like that.

And here is the thing I had not let myself expect: she was still there. The network was gone, the leash was gone, the whole apparatus that had owned my mind was gone — and behind my own walls, untouched, was my sister's face, exactly where I had left it. They had never once been able to reach that room. Cutting myself off from them cost me everything and took nothing from her. Whatever I lost on that floor in the dark, I had not lost Elena. I checked. She was there. She is always there.

I walked away before morning. I have been walking ever since.

They call me an apostate now — AWOL, a recovery file with my own name on it, open like Elena's, and like hers it will never be actioned, because they have never once been fast enough. I am not free of them in the way the word *free* would like to mean. I am hunted, and alone, and the silence where the lattice used to be has never entirely filled in.

But I am off the leash. My mind is my own. And for the first time since I was twelve, the thing wearing my sister is no longer somebody else's line item, deferred forever on a list I am not on.

It is mine.
