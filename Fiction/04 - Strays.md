# Strays

*The fourth of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

For the first weeks after I cut myself loose, I was very bad at being a person.

I had been a node in a machine that knew where I was every moment, that fed and housed and scheduled me, that told me what I was for. I had never bought my own food with my own money in a city that did not care whether I lived or died. I did not know how to be nobody. The institution teaches you a great many things. It does not teach you how to be poor, because it never intends to let you go.

So I was clumsy at it, and frightened, and I made the mistakes you make: I slept where I shouldn't, asked questions the wrong way, held myself like a man expecting a hand on his shoulder. I was hunting, even then — the thing wearing my sister had gone to ground, and a thing that hides in the overlooked places can only be found by living in them — but I did not yet understand that the overlooked places are not empty. I thought I had walked off the map. I had only walked onto a different one, drawn by different people, and I could not read it yet.

Doreen taught me to read it.

She ran a coffee operation out of a cart and a folding table on a corner the city had given up on, four in the morning, the hour that belongs to nobody. I had been circling that block for two nights, badly. The third night she set a paper cup down in front of me without my asking and said, "You've got a look. Not the junkie look. Not the running-from-a-man look. The other one. Like you used to belong to something, and it spat you out, and now you don't know what your hands are for." Then she went back to her cart, because she had said the true thing and saw no reason to stand there while it landed.

I knew what she was within a minute. There is a particular weight to the old and the masterless of the spirit world, and she carried it lightly, the way only the very old do. Bone Gnawer. The tribe the rest of her kind look past — the ones with no high forest, no proud hall, only the cracks of the city and what grows in them. I had been raised by an institution that believed information was something you extracted from above, through machines, at scale. Doreen believed something else, and she was right.

"You're looking for somebody," she said, the second night I came back. Not a question. "Everybody down here is looking for somebody or hiding from somebody. The difference is, we notice. You people up top think the street's empty." She gestured with her chin at the dark beyond the cart's little light — the doorways, the lots, the figures the city had taught itself not to see. "It's the most crowded place there is. You want to find a thing that doesn't want to be found, you don't go where the cameras are. You come down here, where the people the world threw away sit watching every single thing go by, all night, because what else is there to do."

That was the whole education. I have built ten years of work on it. The thing wearing Elena has to eat, has to sleep, has to move through the world on someone's streets — and the only eyes that truly watch the streets belong to the people no one watches back. I stopped looking up. I learned to look down here, and down here is where I have caught every true thread I have ever caught.

I kept waiting for the price. I had become, by then, a man who understood the world as a ledger — I did jobs for rent, cleanup work, the quiet kind, and everyone I dealt with wanted something and named it. So I asked Doreen what she wanted. She looked at me like I'd said something a little sad. "When one of mine needs the kind of thing a man like you can do," she said, "you'll do it. And you won't be strange about it. That's all. Nobody down here keeps a tab that long." And that was the price, and it was nothing, and the nothing is what nearly took me apart.

Because here is what I had not been ready for: she was about the age my mother would be. She had that flat, unsentimental kindness that does not ask to be thanked. And for one half of one second, sitting in the warmth off her little burner with a coffee I had not earned, I felt a door begin to open that I had welded shut two years before, and I shut it again, hard, before anything could come through. I did not tell her my name that night. I never told her about Elena. Not that night, not in all the nights after.

That is the truth about me and the strays, and I will set it down plainly because it is not flattering: I loved them, in the careful way I have left, and I never let a single one of them close enough to be hurt by it. Getting near me has a cost. I have watched it get paid. So I sat at their fires and I took their threads and I did their quiet jobs when they asked, and I kept the wall up the whole time, and the wall was the kindest thing I had to give. You keep people out of the ledger by keeping them out. Doreen, I think, understood that better than I did. She never once asked me in.

Wherever I slept that week — a shelter cot, a bus bench, the back of a job that ran late — I did the thing I do. I went to the cold quiet place where I keep my sister and I held her face until it was steady, and then I slept. I had learned something that mattered, that night at the cart: that I could sit in the warm and still go home to the cold, and that the two would never touch, because I would not let them. A man can be near a fire his whole life and never once let it thaw the one room he needs to stay frozen.

I have been a stray ever since, among strays. It is the closest thing to a pack I will allow myself, which is to say it is not one. But they let me sit at the fire. After everything, that turned out to be a great deal.
