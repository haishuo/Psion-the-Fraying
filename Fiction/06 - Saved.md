# Saved

*The sixth of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

I did not go there to be saved. I went there for a name.

The Railroad had something I needed — a route, a forged history, a way to move a body across two states without the lattice ever lighting up — and I had learned by then that they would trade it, more or less, for the chance to look at me with that particular expression they have, the one that is two parts hope and one part grief. I told myself it was a transaction like any other. I was wrong, but only a little, and only in the way that matters.

They keep one of their boltholes inside a caern — Children of Gaia, the gentlest of a not-gentle people — and the place hums with the kind of spirit-weight that lifts the hair on a Detection man's arms. But that is not what I remember. What I remember is the children.

They were everywhere. Pulled out of the same machine that pulled me out, except *in time* — extracted before the binding, before the leash, before the kind voices ever reached their parents. Saved. They had names nobody had erased. Their parents were gone, corrected into forgetting, but the children at least did not have to be no one. They were learning to brace from people who loved them instead of people who owned them. They were arguing about dinner. One of them was crying because another had broken something, an ordinary catastrophe, the kind I have not had since I was twelve.

I stood in the middle of all that salvation and understood, with the cold clarity I bring to everything now, that I was looking at the life I did not get. Not bitterly — I am past bitter. Just clearly. There is a version of me who came up through a room like this, learned his walls from someone gentle, and grew into a person instead of a purpose. I will never meet him. But I saw his classroom.

The conductor's name was Jonah, and he was good at his work, which is to say he did not insult me by pretending the pitch was anything but a pitch. He let me have my route. Then he sat across from me and said the thing none of the others have ever quite had the nerve to say to my face.

"You don't have to spend yourself on this," he said. "There's a version where you put it down. Not forget her — nobody's asking you to forget her — just stop carrying the whole weight alone until it kills you. We could use you. Not as a weapon. As a person. We've got people who know what you know and sleep at night anyway. You could be one of them." He paused, and then, gently, which was the cruel part: "You talk about the end of this like it's a death you already agreed to. I've buried a lot of people who agreed to their deaths. I'd rather not bury one who's still arguing with me across a table."

He was not wrong about a single word, and that is exactly why I could not let him keep opening the door he was opening. Because the truth — the one I do not say to the kind ones, because it would only hurt them — is that I cannot afford a future. If I let myself believe in the version of me who puts it down and sleeps at night, I will start to want him, and a man who wants to live is a man who will hesitate at the one moment he must not. My usefulness, my whole terrible usefulness, is that I have already spent the part of me that flinches. Save that part and you ruin the tool. I need to *not know* who I would be afterward. The not-knowing is load-bearing.

So I thanked him, and I meant it, and I gave him the truth I could afford instead of the one I couldn't: that I was not a man who could be saved, only a man who could finish a thing, and that he should spend his hope on the children, who could still use it. He looked at me a long moment, the hope draining out of the expression until only the grief was left. Then he let me go. They always let me go. It is the worst thing about them and the best.

On the way out I passed a girl of about eight, sitting on a step, fierce over some small wrong — and for one moment my chest did the thing I do not let it do, because she was the age Elena will always be, and she was *here,* among the saved, and my sister was not and never would be, and the arithmetic of that is the one sum I cannot make come out clean no matter how many times I run it.

I had my route. I had my refusal. I was three steps from the tree line and gone.

And then something snagged.

I read people the way other men breathe — I do not decide to, it simply happens, every mind near me registering at the edge of my attention like warmth, like weather. The caern was loud with it: the children bright and unguarded, Jonah's steady grief, the slow deep presence of the place itself. All of it legible. All of it *there.*

Except for one.

There was a spot, off to my left, down near the water, where my senses reported a person and could tell me nothing else. Not a wall — I know walls; I build the best ones there are. Not silence, either. Something stranger: a person-shaped patch of *wrong,* a note played at a pitch my whole life had never taught me to hear, a hole in the legible world exactly the size and shape of someone standing in it.

I had never, not once since the night my power woke, met a mind I could not read.

I should have kept walking. The route was in my pocket and the trees were right there.

I turned toward the water instead. Some things you cannot not do.
