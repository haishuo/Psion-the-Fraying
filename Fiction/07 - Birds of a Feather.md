# Birds of a Feather

*The seventh of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

The wrongness got worse the nearer I came to it, the way a sound you can't place gets worse, and I understood before I reached the water that I was not walking toward a *thing* but toward a *person* — and that the person was the source of it, and that this should not have been possible. A place can be warded. A mind can be walled. But a mind reads as a mind even behind the best walls in the world — I would know — the way a locked house still reads as a house. This read as nothing. This read as a house-shaped patch of fog.

She was sitting on a rock at the edge of the water with her knees drawn up, maybe seventeen, watching me come the way you watch weather you have decided not to fear. And the instant I was close enough to do the thing I do without deciding to — the soft automatic brush of attention I lay on every living person, the one I have never once had to think about — it slid off her and found nothing to hold. Water off stone. I did it again, harder, before I could stop myself, the way you press a tooth that has begun to ache.

"That doesn't work on me," she said. "Nothing like that does. You can stop pushing — you'll only give yourself a headache." A beat. "And it's rude."

I want to convey what that was like, because I have spent my life since I was twelve being the one who *knows* — the one who walks into a room already holding everyone in it. I read people the way other men read road signs; it is the deepest habit I have, deeper than grief, nearly as deep as the ritual. And here was a girl on a rock who returned my oldest reflex precisely nothing, who sat entirely outside the only sense I fully trust. I did not feel powerful near her. I felt, for the first time in a decade, blind in one eye and aware of it.

She had read *me* long before I failed to read her. "You're one of the mind ones," she said. Not a question. "Not a lattice one, though. You've got the look of somebody who left." Her head tilted. "They send you? No — they wouldn't send somebody like you. They'd send somebody who still believed them." She decided something, and the wariness in her shoulders came down half an inch, which I would learn, in time, was as far as it ever came down for anyone.

I had no read on her, so I had to do the thing I almost never do with strangers: actually talk to her, and watch her face, and guess, like a person who cannot cheat. It was slow, and clumsy, and strange. I am not used to guessing. I found I did not entirely hate it.

She told me, when I asked, that the people who knew what she was had a great many words for her, and not one of them was her name. One set called her an anomaly — a glitch in a ledger. Another set, her mother's people, called her an abomination, which at least had the honesty of hatred in it. "Everybody works out *what* I am before they ever get to *who*," she said, with a flatness that had had the self-pity worn off it years before. "You're the first one who couldn't manage the first part." She searched for the word. "It's restful."

I knew exactly what she meant. I have spent ten years as a case the institutions deferred forever, a recovery action that never came. They have a word for me too, and it is not Marcus. So we sat there, the two of us, in the particular quiet of people the system could not make fit anywhere, and for a little while neither of us was a category. I have no better word for what passed between us than *recognition.* Two of a kind, by each being one of a kind. Birds of a feather, where the feather is *unclassifiable.*

She could tell I was carrying something — anyone can; I am only invisible to people who don't look — and she did not ask me what. I could tell she was carrying something the size of a life, and I did not ask her either. We had both, I think, had things taken out of us by people who reached in without asking, and it makes you careful with other people's doors. She did not try to read me, though I suspect in her strange way she could have. I could not try to read her at all. It came to the same courtesy from opposite directions, and we both noticed, and neither of us said so.

"Eve," she told me, when at last I asked the only thing that mattered. Not the anomaly. Not the abomination. The name she had given herself, against everything the world had tried to call her instead. I told her mine — the real one, which I give to almost no one, and which felt, for once, like a fair trade and not a risk.

I had my route. I had a thing to do that left no room for whatever this was. So I left, the way I leave everyone, before any of it could take hold. But I carried her out with me — the girl I could not read, the proof that I was not the only thing the machine had failed to digest.

I did not know yet, sitting by that water, what she would come to mean to me — nor what one impossible fact about her, a fact I would not learn for years, would be worth on the worst and last night of all of this. I knew only that I had met a person I could not *know,* and that it had felt, against all my training, like being handed something rather than having something taken.

It is a rare gift, to meet someone and be made to *ask.* I have not been given it often. I have never forgotten the first time.
