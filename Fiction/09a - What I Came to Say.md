# What I Came to Say

*The ninth of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. My account of the night whose other half is [Excavation](09b%20-%20Excavation.md), told from Isolde's side. The same memory, two keepers. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

I went to her the same night I understood what she had handed me.

I had it out of her own hands, in a manner of speaking — though neither of us knew that then. She had hired me that evening for what looked like any other cleanup: a witness gone stale, a memory she'd papered over years back that had started to bleed through the paper. Routine. I lifted it clean, filed it with the thousand others I keep, and had not yet named a fee when I set that erased night beside the map I hold of every face the thing has worn and understood what she had handed me. The witness had watched a woman drain a clerk dead by the harbor, on the ninth of October eleven years gone — and the clerk was a face on my map, a host the thing had worn before it ever wore my sister's. The same one. She had paid me to bury the last proof of the worst night of her unlife, and I had buried it, and only minutes afterward read what I was burying. It had been her wound before it was mine.

I did not sit on it. Every year of careful distance I had kept with her told me to finish the job, take the fee, and let a decent interval stand between what I now knew and what I was about to say to a four-hundred-year-old Ventrue about the worst night of her unlife. I would have obeyed that instinct for anything ordinary — I have refused smaller doors than this one for years, and called the refusal caution. But going to her that night meant going *not for a job,* and I did not have ten years left to spend working up to it, because this was not a case I could set down and come back to. It was the one line on my map I had spent a decade waiting to find. I opened the door I had kept shut for years because, for once, keeping it shut cost more than opening it.

In the end I went because I was tired of being the only one who knew. Not about Elena — about the *shape* of the thing: the soullessness, the no-one-home, the fact that there is nothing in there to hate. I have spent a decade explaining that to people who flinch from it. The Inquisition needs it to be a containment problem; the Projects need it to be a soul you can still save; everyone needs it to be something it is not, and I am so tired of arguing the truth uphill. And across the city was a woman who had *met* it — who had had the nothing reach into her and prove itself — and who would never once ask me to save the host, because she knew better than anyone living or dead that there is no host left to save. I did not go for help. I went because I needed, just once, to say the true thing to someone who would not need convincing.

So I told her. Flat, the way you set a bone — that the night she had spent eleven years calling madness or weakness was neither; that a dying thing had reached into her and dragged a memory to the surface, and that the corpse she woke to was not the one crack in four centuries of iron control but a hand forced from outside: it had spent her discipline for her, drained the reins out of her grip, and made her finish a man she'd meant only to sip and send home forgetting. It was not her fault and had not even been *about* her. I gave her the cruelest gift I own, which is the truth: that there was no malice in it because there was no one in it, and that this is worse, every time, because it takes away the villain and leaves you only the wound. My own people would have done the reverse. They would have reached in and smoothed the whole night flat and called it mercy. I have come to believe the hard true thing is the only real kindness I have left in me. It is the one thing they never gave me.

And then she dared me to look. *See what it left,* she said — half surrender, half test — and held out the worst night of four centuries like a man baring his throat.

I have thought, since, about how fast the *no* came. There was nothing to decide. To reach into her — even gently, even invited, even to *help* — is the one thing I will not do, because it is the thing the world tries to do to me every day, and the thing that creature did to her, and the thing my own institution did to my mother with a clean conscience and a kind voice. I spend every night of my life holding a single door shut against exactly that. I was not going to open someone else's and pretend it was a different act because my hands were warm. Her memory was hers. I told her so. I have given little in my life that I am proud of. I am proud of that *no.*

To make her understand why it has to die, I had to show her the one thing I show no one. I told her about Elena — the eight years of her; that there are no photographs, because they corrected the people who would have kept them, tidied my sister out of the world like a closed account; that I am the last place she still exists. I said the truest sentence I own, the one I had never said aloud before that night and have not since: that the thing has Elena's face, and I have *Elena.*

I had come to be the one doing the seeing. I had not planned to be seen. But you cannot hand someone that and stay furniture, and when I looked up she was looking at me — *really* looking, the way she had never looked in three years of paying me like a man who comes to bleed the radiators — and I felt the precise thing I have spent a decade making certain no one could do to me. I felt *known.* It was terrible. It was also, and I had not braced for this, a relief so large I had to hold very still inside it so it would not show.

I had come to give and leave. Instead she gave something back — the one resource I cannot manufacture, which is *time.* She has all the years I do not. She offered her eyes, her names, her bottomless money, and then — she did not finish the sentence, and I did not make her — to keep looking, *after.* After me. No one had ever offered to outlast my death on Elena's behalf. I had built my entire life so that no one could.

I said yes. I am single-minded, not proud; the hunt is everything, and an immortal who understands is worth more than my dignity. And then, because the size of the thing needed a floor under it before one of us said something neither could take back, I gave her the smallest true thing I had: that I'd still be taking the next job whenever she had one. Rent doesn't stop for any of this.

I went to her house to tell her a single fact. I left having told her all of it — Elena, the no-one-home, the missing photographs, the *no,* the entire shape of me — and having been, for the length of one conversation, not the only one who knew. Not, for once, alone with it.

I had not understood how heavy *only* was until someone helped me set it down for an hour.

I have not let myself get used to it. Closeness has a price; I have watched it get paid. But I walked out of that house altered in one exact way I have never managed to undo: there is a single person in the world I can no longer hand a fee and turn from.

I came to say one thing. *It lived.* I did not know, going in, that I was also the one who needed to be found.
