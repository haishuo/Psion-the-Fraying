# Excavation

*Companion fiction for Psion: The Fraying — not rules, not required reading. It illustrates two things the canon states more drily: what a psidolon can do to a Kindred mind ("Exposure Without Infection," in [Psidolons — The Untethered](../Setting/Psidolons%20-%20The%20Untethered.md)), and the alliance between [Marcus Thiel](../People/Marcus%20Thiel.md) and the Ventrue Isolde. The night he tells her it lived.*

---

The psion had cleaned up after her three times, and Isolde had never once thought of him as a person.

This was not cruelty; it was economy. One does not think of the locksmith as a person, or the man who comes to bleed the radiators. He was simply *useful* — and, better, useful without the price the others charged. When a childe left a witness babbling, or a feeding went too far in too public a place, the MetaConcert would clean it, yes, but the MetaConcert was an institution, and institutions remember what they are owed. This one remembered nothing and asked for nothing but his fee. He arrived, he made the problem cease to have happened, he named a flat and reasonable price in money or a favor, and he left. Three times. Cold, cordial, finished. She paid him the way one pays a tradesman, and thought of him, when she thought of him at all, the way one thinks of a good tradesman: with the mild approval owed a thing that works.

So when he asked to see her — not for a job; he had been specific that it was not for a job — she received him out of a curiosity so faint it was nearly annoyance, and expected something tedious. A renegotiation of rates. Some mortal difficulty he wanted her money or her name to solve.

He sat across the low table from her and declined, with a small courteous tilt of the head, the wine he could not drink. And she saw, for the first time in three years of dealings, that he was tired — not sleepy, but *spent*, a man who had carried something heavy so long the weight had become his posture. It had never once occurred to her, in three years, that the work she hired him for was only what he did to eat.

"You said this wasn't a job," she said.

"It isn't." He set a folded paper on the table and did not push it toward her. "You've used me three times. You've never asked what I do the rest of the time — no reason you would. I'm going to tell you, because it turns out to concern you." A pause. "Eleven years ago, on the ninth of October, you fed on a man near the harbor. A clerk. Unremarkable. You meant to take a little and make him forget. He died instead."

The room did not change. The candles did not gutter. Isolde had four centuries of practice at letting nothing show, and nothing showed. But somewhere beneath the still water of her face, very far down, something she had spent eleven years drowning turned over in its sleep.

"You're mistaken," she said.

"I'm not." No heat in it. He was the way a doctor is, naming a thing you've decided not to have. "Something happened to you that night you've never told anyone, because no one would believe it, and because believing it yourself is worse. You've spent eleven years deciding it didn't happen — because the only other possibility is that a dying mortal reached into you and did *that*, and that isn't allowed to be true."

"How," she said, and stopped, because her voice had done something she had not permitted.

"I read it," he said. "Not from you — I've never once looked inside you. You were a client; I don't read the furniture." A breath. "You've hired me three times. Two were nothing — a childe's spill, a feeding too public to leave standing. The third you called a stale witness: a memory you'd bent shut years ago that had started coming back. You didn't say whose night it was. You didn't know it was any different from the other two — and neither did I, then. I lifted it, filed it, and hadn't yet taken your money when I set it beside the rest of what I keep and saw the face in it. The man by the harbor. I'd been hunting the thing that wore him for ten years." He paused. "You paid me to bury the last witness to the worst night of your unlife, and I buried it well, and came here within the hour of putting it in the ground. I came because I think you've spent eleven years believing you went mad, or were weak, or were fooled by a corpse. You weren't. None of those things happened."

She wanted to tell him to leave. She could not make the words.

"It's called a psidolon," he said. "What's left when one of my kind breaks past the last limit and doesn't die. No body of its own; it wears people. And it has no soul — that's the part that matters, because there's no *one* in there. When you fed on its host, you were killing the only thing keeping it tethered, and it did what a drowning thing does. It grabbed — reached for the nearest mind to climb into, and the nearest mind was yours, and the Beast wouldn't let it in. But the reaching churned something up, and it brought what it found to the surface. That's all. It didn't choose what to show you. It didn't know it was showing anything. It found a memory near the top of you and it *pulled*."

"The sun," she said. She had not meant to.

"Yes."

She was aware, distantly, of her own hands, folded and perfectly still. "It gave me the sun," she said. "Do you understand what — no. You can't. You walk in it." She heard the cruelty in her voice and did not soften it. "It gave me my village. My mother's house. It let me believe — for a day, it *felt* like a day — that I had dreamed all of this. The four hundred years. That I was a girl, and alive, and none of it had happened yet." Her jaw set. "And then it showed me the night they came. Made me watch them take me again. And I woke with a dead man at my feet and no way to know if I'd imagined any of it."

"You didn't imagine it. And the dead man wasn't your hand slipping." He gave it to her plainly, the way he gave her everything. "You meant to take a little and send him off forgetting. It spent you — drained the control out of you the way you were draining him — and made you finish. Four centuries without a lapse, and the once you broke, it wasn't you that broke. Something reached in and broke you."

"*Why*," she said. The word came out of her like something pulled. "What did it want from me. What did it *get*."

He looked away — down at the folded paper he had not opened — and was silent long enough that she understood he was choosing his words the way you handle a thing that can cut.

"Nothing," he said. "It got nothing. There was nothing it wanted. That's the part no one can stand, so I'll say it plainly and you can hate me for it: it didn't do it *to* you. You weren't there, to it. It would have done the same to a chair, if a chair had a memory in it. There was no malice because there was no one in it to have any." He met her eyes. "I know that's worse. It lands that way every time. If it hated you, you'd have something — a reason, someone to be the villain. This took the villain away and left you the wound."

It was the truest and most unbearable thing anyone had said to her in two hundred years. She had wanted, all this time, something to *hate*, and not knowing what had been the splinter she could never work out, because hatred needs an object and the object had been a corpse and a story she couldn't tell. Now this exhausted tradesman had handed her the object and taken it away in the same breath. There was nothing to hate. There had never been anyone there.

"You could see it," she said, after a while. "The memory. You said you collect them."

"Yes."

"Then look." It came out as a dare, or a surrender; she could not have said which. "See what it left. Everyone should know what it is to —"

"No." He said it gently, and it stopped her more completely than force would have. "I won't look. You don't have to show me anything." He turned the folded paper over once in his fingers, an old habit, something he did when he was holding still. "I spend every night of my life keeping one memory safe from people who'd reach in and take it. I know what that thing did to you. I'm not going to do a kinder version of it and call it help. Your memory is yours. I came to give you something — not take more."

She had been braced — braced from the moment he said the word *read* — for him to be like the other thing. A mind that reached. And he had reached toward the door instead, and closed it.

"What did you come to give me," she said.

He was quiet. Then: "It didn't die that night. I'm sorry. You've spent eleven years thinking it died with the man you drank. It got out — into someone on the street, before you'd finished — and it's been wearing people ever since. For the last ten years it's worn one." Something moved through his stillness, quickly, and was put away. "Her name was Elena. My sister. She was eight."

"You have a photograph," she said. Not quite a question. People carried photographs.

"No." Very quiet. "There aren't any. When my own people brought me in, they corrected everyone who'd have kept one — my mother, my father. Tidied her out of the world for operational security, the way you'd close an account." His hand rose almost to his temple and stopped. "I'm the only place she still exists. The thing you met is wearing her grown face somewhere out there tonight, and I have her the way she was, in here, and nowhere else. That's the whole shape of it. It has her face. I have *her*."

The candles burned. Far off, the city made its small night sounds. And Isolde — who had paid this man a tradesman's fee three times and turned her mind from him the instant the door closed — understood, all at once, that he had been carrying this the whole time. Through every clean and cordial transaction. Scrubbing her careless mess from a witness's mind to make the rent, with a dead sister behind his eyes and a decade of hunt in his shoulders. She had hired the most haunted man in the city to bleed her radiators, and never looked at him once.

"You're going to kill it," she said.

"I'm going to unmake it. There's a difference, with these. You can't kill the host without it jumping; you take the thing apart — its memories, all of them, every face it's worn, until there isn't enough left to hold together." He said it the way other men recite a prayer worn smooth. "It'll take everything I have. It may take me. I've made my peace with that."

"And you came to *me*."

"You're my nearest thing to a friend in your world, and that's a cold word for what we are, I know. But it's the warmest one available." Almost an apology. "And in ten years you're the only one I've found who already understands why it has to be done. Everyone else, I have to convince. The Inquisition would call it a containment problem. The Projects would ask if there's a way to save the host. There isn't. There hasn't been for years. What I'm hunting isn't Elena. You don't need me to explain that — you *met* it. You're the one person alive or dead who'd never ask me to try."

She was silent a long time, thinking of the sun she would never see, that the thing had given her and taken; and of the tired man across the table, who could not give her back the sun, who could give her nothing she actually wanted, who had walked into her house with the one offering that was his to make. Not vengeance — he'd been honest, there was no vengeance to be had, no one to avenge it on. Only its absence from the world. Only that it would, someday, by this man's hand, simply *stop*.

A small thing. A human-sized thing, mortal and finite, against a wound as long as she was.

"I have resources," Isolde said at last, surprised by how steady the words came. "Names. Eyes. Money that doesn't end. You've done this alone for ten years on a mortal's lifespan, taking cleanup jobs to keep the lights on." The smallest edge of the old elegance returned to her voice, because it was that or something she would not show him. "I have all the time you don't. Tell me where you've looked. And if it outlasts you — I'll keep looking after you're —"

She did not finish. He did not make her.

"All right," he said. Then, after a moment, with the ghost of something that on a less tired man might have been humor: "I'll still have to take the next job, when you have one. Rent doesn't stop for any of this."

"I know," she said. And found, to her own quiet astonishment, that she would never again be able to hand him a tradesman's fee and turn away.

Neither of them knew — neither could have known — that the art his people would one night be hunted for, the command of the mind her clan counted among its oldest birthrights, had passed from his kind to hers in a bargain struck ten thousand years before either was born. They believed they were two victims who had found each other. They were also the heir and the inheritor of the same stolen thing, bent over the same stolen face. But that is another story, and the dead keep their secrets even from themselves.
