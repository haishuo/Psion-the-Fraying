# Room and Board

*The tenth of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

For six weeks, nothing happened.

People imagine a hunt as movement. It is mostly waiting for someone else to make a mistake, and there are stretches when no one obliges. The thing wearing Elena had gone quiet. The Virtual Adept who watched the records I could not touch had nothing. The Glass Walker who watched the roads had nothing. Cherub had heard three rumors, sold me two, and refunded one after it proved to be a woman in Ohio with the wrong eyes and an ordinary soul. Even paying work had stopped arriving. Apparently there are seasons in which nobody needs a witness cleaned, a nightmare found, or an inconvenient hour lifted out of a head.

Rent, unlike the supernatural world, remained admirably consistent.

I was twenty-one, eleven dollars short of paying for the room through Friday, and considering whether the landlord would accept precision memory work performed on one of his better evenings when Doreen sent me an address in Somerset County and five words.

*Witches. One of mine. Paying.*

I called her from the pay phone downstairs.

"How paying?"

"Good morning to you too."

"Good morning. How paying?"

"Not very."

I began to tell her no.

"You remember what I said when you first came around my cart?" she asked.

I remembered everything Doreen had ever said around that cart. She knew it.

"When one of mine needs the kind of thing you can do, you'll do it, and you won't be strange about it." She let that sit for half a breath. "One of mine needs it. And you're already being strange."

The people at the address were not Bone Gnawers. They were Verbena: four witches on an old farm in the Laurel Highlands, useful to Doreen's people because they treated whoever came to the door and did not ask whether an ordinary hospital would approve. One of them had been taken by the Technocratic Union and returned in pieces no Life-working could reach. Doreen thought a Memory specialist might find the seams.

"They can feed you," she added. "Might improve your disposition."

"My disposition is stable."

"That's what I'm worried about."

I took the job because I had given Doreen my word years before. I took it because someone had been hurt by an institution that called violation treatment, and I have never required much persuasion on that subject. I took it because eleven dollars is eleven dollars.

Those were all true reasons. At that point they were even all the reasons I had.

The Laurel Hill Circle lived in a stone-and-timber farmhouse on eighteen acres of orchard, garden, spring, and repairs deferred one season too many. I called it a cottage in my head, which was inaccurate but less dangerous. A cottage is a place one visits. A farmhouse has rooms, work, people returning at evening. A farmhouse begins making claims.

I knocked. The door opened.

She had dark curls, brown eyes, dirt on one cheek, and a kitchen knife in her hand.

Not raised. She had been cutting something when I arrived. I include that because later descriptions of Leah tend to become either more romantic or more alarming than the moment deserves. She was not a forest apparition armed for blood sacrifice. She was a twenty-year-old woman interrupted while making lunch, though she was also, as it happened, entirely capable of blood sacrifice.

"Marcus Thiel?" she asked.

I had a speech. I always had a speech. It established the professional nature of the arrangement, the limits of my access, the client's right to end the work, the fee, the risks, and the fact that employment did not purchase loyalty beyond the stated terms. I had given it to Princes, Adepts, grieving parents, liars, and men who could remove my head with one hand. I could have recited it while concussed.

I could not remember its first word.

She waited.

"Yes," I said eventually.

"Is that all?"

"No."

It was, for several more seconds.

Her expression settled into a doubt so complete it became almost peaceful. "Doreen said you were good."

"I am."

"At what?"

That was how I met Leah Mercer.

Nora Ellis was twenty-nine and looked older when I first saw her, not because suffering carries any honest relationship to age but because the New World Order had taught her body to expect the next instruction before the present moment had finished. She watched doors. She lost the thread when a vehicle slowed outside. Certain ordinary words made her attention go still in ways ordinary fear did not explain.

Leah brought me into the front room and stood between Nora and me without seeming to have chosen the position.

"You don't touch her mind until I know exactly what that means," she said.

"Leah," Nora said from the chair behind her.

"I'm asking."

"You're answering."

Leah looked back. Nora's hands were shaking, but her voice was not.

"He explains it to me," Nora said. "I decide. You can dislike him afterward."

Leah moved aside.

That was the first useful thing I learned about the house. They protected one another fiercely and did not confuse protection with ownership — not for long, at least, and not after being corrected by the person whose life it was.

I gave Nora the speech I had failed to give at the door. Leah listened to every word with her arms folded. Nora asked better questions.

The NWO had not merely interrogated her. Nora had been the house's connection to everything beyond the mountain: safe places, message routes, names, the quiet network by which people who could not advertise found one another. They had taken the map from her, altered it, and then sent it walking home.

She had not escaped. Her memory of the escape was too complete.

Real memory is wasteful. It keeps the color of a wall and loses the sentence that changed your life. It remembers weather no one noticed and puts a thumb through the center of terror. The route the NWO had left Nora was immaculate: every corridor placed, every turn justified, every guard conveniently looking elsewhere. It was not a memory. It was documentation wearing one.

Under it I found conditioned associations, reporting compulsions, false recognitions, and instructions disguised as her own relief. A road name could make her want to call a number she did not remember learning. The sight of one kind of utility vehicle opened a route toward everything she had heard in the previous hour. Familiar places had been made into switches. They had returned her because a frightened liaison going home would draw a better map than a prisoner ever could.

I told Nora what I found as I found it. She chose what we touched and when. There were structures I could cut cleanly, structures we had to approach by degrees, and injuries for which the honest professional answer was *I do not yet know.* I used that answer often.

Leah distrusted me for the first four days.

On the fifth, a trigger collapsed halfway through removal and left Nora unable to distinguish the room from the place the NWO had built behind her eyes. I held the route open. Leah held Nora: slowed her breathing, brought her body down from a panic whose cause her body could not know was foreign, and kept speaking to her in the ordinary present tense until the present became available again.

Afterward, while Nora slept, Leah found me sitting on the back step with my hands shaking.

"Did you hurt her?" she asked.

"Yes."

She sat beside me, not close. "Was there another way?"

"Not through that part."

"Did she know?"

"Yes."

"Did she choose it?"

"Yes."

Leah nodded once. She disappeared into the kitchen and returned with tea and bread. She did not tell me I had done the right thing. A person can consent to necessary pain and the pain remains pain. She understood that better than most people who have opinions about healing.

That was when she began trusting me.

I cannot identify when I began trusting her. Memory is not the same as understanding, and an exact record offers no protection against missing the event while it happens.

The formal work took sixteen days. At the end Nora still startled at engines and lost time when she was tired. She also knew which thoughts were hers. She could walk through the orchard without carrying everyone in the house toward an NWO file. She could hear the road names that had been made into commands and recognize the foreign hand in them. That was healing. It was not being put back the way she had been, because people are not saved copies and injury is not an error you reverse.

I explained this badly over dinner on the seventeenth night.

"I don't fix minds," I said. "Memory is routes. I found the roads they built, closed the ones that went where they wanted, and reopened some of hers. The damage is still—"

"A scar," Leah said.

"Yes."

"You do for the mind what I do for the body."

I looked at her.

"Was that not what you were going to say?"

It was, once she had said it.

The Circle paid me what it could. The sum did not cover the rent I had already lost, much less the next month. This should have annoyed me. Instead I explained that the account remained imbalanced, that room and board had cash value, that the barn roof leaked, that one boundary fence was listing into the trees, and that the old boiler produced a sound no responsible person could ignore.

Nora looked at Leah. Leah looked at me.

"How long do you think the fence will take?" she asked.

"That depends on the posts."

"Of course it does."

I stayed to settle the account.

The fence took two days.

I found other liabilities.

There was always work at the house, which was one of the things I came to love about it before I admitted I loved anything there. Work ended. A repaired hinge swung correctly. Split wood became a stack. A row planted in the morning existed by supper. No one moved the objective when I approached it or told me the timetable belonged to another department. The roof did not lie about leaking. The boiler was hostile but straightforward.

Leah worked beside me without making my usefulness ceremonial. She knew more about the garden, the animals, the spring, and every living thing on the property. I knew how to make a fence line straight and how to approach a machine that had been repaired badly by six different people. We disagreed about methods. She was right more often on her ground and gracious about it less often than she believed.

She had no Mind at all, in the language mages use. She did not need it. People confuse access with understanding. I can enter a memory and still fail to know why its owner kept it. Leah could stand beside a person, leave every door closed, and notice what they were trying not to ask.

The first time she kissed me, I was explaining why replacing the barn latch had to be counted against the unpaid balance.

It was not a long kiss. It was long enough to remove the number from my head.

"You can put that in the ledger," she said.

"Under what category?"

She laughed at me and went back to the house.

I did not follow for almost a minute, which I considered a meaningful display of caution.

At night I still performed the ritual. Same hour, same sequence, Elena's face restored against every false adult expression the creature had worn in the day's leads. Leah once found me afterward beneath the apple trees. She had seen enough to know I had gone somewhere she was not invited.

"Her name was Elena," she said.

I had not told her. I must have said the name aloud during the ritual without knowing she was near enough to hear. There were ordinary explanations.

"Yes."

"Do you want to tell me about her?"

"No."

"All right."

She sat with me anyway. She did not ask again that night. This was more intimate than asking would have been.

By the seventh week I had a place at the table no one else used. There were seedlings in the garden I had planted and would not be present to harvest, though I did not phrase it that way. Leah left a cup beside me in the mornings without asking whether I wanted one. Nora had begun carrying messages again, short distances at first and always by her own choice. The barn no longer leaked in two of the four places it had leaked before.

I was happy.

I did not know this. I know it now because I remember the exact instant it ended.

Cherub called shortly after dusk.

He did not waste words. A woman matching the grown projection of my drawing had used one of three names at a bus station east of Pittsburgh. A ghoul had watched her leave with a man who asked the same question twice and did not recognize her the second time. Cherub had a route, a destination purchased from someone expensive, and enough corroboration that he was charging me rather than offering rumor.

The first thing I felt was irritation.

For ten years a true lead had been the nearest thing I possessed to hope. I had slept in stations, bled for strangers, and sold pieces of my gift to monsters for the next one. Cherub handed me what I had built my entire life to receive, and I resented him because supper was on the table and Leah's hand had been resting against my shoulder.

The feeling lasted perhaps a second.

Then: Elena.

The house became temporary again. The seedlings became things I would not see grown. The chair became furniture belonging to someone else. I began arranging departure before Cherub finished naming his price.

When I turned, Leah was watching me.

"You found her," she said.

"It."

She absorbed the correction without flinching. "You found it."

"A probable route. I need to leave tonight."

There were many things she could have said. The selfish ones would not have been wrong. She was twenty, and in love, and I had spent a month behaving as though the account between us explained why I woke wanting to see her. Some part of her wanted to tell me to stay. I know because she never became dishonest merely by becoming kind.

"I don't want you to go," she said.

I had no defense ready for the sentence. She spared me from constructing one.

"That isn't the same as asking you to stay."

"Leah—"

"You have to go." Her voice broke on *have* and steadied afterward. "This was here before me. You won't be able to live in this house while part of you is still walking every road looking for it. So go. Finish it."

She could have turned herself into another obligation. *Stay for me.* *Choose me.* *Prove this mattered.* She understood me well enough not to put her love in the shape of a chain.

I packed. The house helped because houses do that when one of their people has to leave: food wrapped, water filled, names offered, objections converted into useful objects. Nora gave me the remaining fragments of what the NWO had known about movement through the region. Leah stood at the door where I had first forgotten how to speak.

She kissed me once. Then she put my hand around the parcel of food I had forgotten to take.

"Go finish it, Marcus."

There was another half to the sentence. She did not say it because saying it would have made a promise she had no right to demand and I was not yet capable of giving.

*Then come home to me.*

At twenty-one, I heard only the first half. I turned toward the hunt because it had called, because Elena was still gone, because I had never built a self that could do anything else.

But for the first time in ten years, I knew exactly where I was leaving.
