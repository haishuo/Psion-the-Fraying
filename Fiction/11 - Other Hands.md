# Other Hands

*The eleventh of the* Memories *— illustrative fiction for Psion: The Fraying, not rules. Marcus Thiel, in his own voice. See [the cycle index](00%20-%20Marcus%20Thiel%20-%20Story%20Arc.md).*

---

Once, on the worst night of my training, I cut the lattice out of my own skull alone in the dark, and told myself afterward that whatever I did from then on, I would do with my own hands. It was the proudest and stupidest vow I ever made. It held right up until I learned that my own death would take my sister with it — and that the one thing I could not do alone had become the only thing I was permitted to do at all.

I cannot unmake the creature by myself anymore. The unmaking is slow; it leaves me open every second of it; and the one mind in the world it still wants is mine. Alone, I die doing it, and if I die, Elena goes out like a snuffed wick. So for the first time since I was twelve years old and a kind woman with a lanyard taught me what trusting an institution costs, I had to put my life in other hands.

I went to find the two pairs that could not be taken from me.

I thought I knew what Isolde was offering. Months earlier, across her low table, she had said she would keep looking after I was gone, and I had filed it the way I file everything — neatly, under *contingency: the hunt continues if I fall.* A patron's promise. Money and time and an immortal's patience, spent on my behalf once I was past spending anything myself.

I was wrong about what she meant, and she let me find that out the way she does everything, without raising her voice.

"When," she said.

"When what?"

"When do we go." Not *you.* *We.* I started to explain — that her part was the long game, the resources, the after — and she looked at me with four centuries of having already decided, and I understood there had never been a version of this where she handed me a sword and waved from a window. "I have waited eleven years to be in the room when that thing stops," she said. "You will not take that from me to spare your pride or my feelings. I will be there. You may decide how useful I am. You do not get to decide whether I come." The faintest edge of the old frost — which I had learned by then was only how she armored something that was not cold at all. "I'm coming. Manage it."

I did not argue. You do not argue with a fact.

Eve was harder, and not because she is hard. Because she would never, ever ask to be let in, and so the door had to be opened from my side, by me, with my own hand — and I have spent my entire life making certain that door does not open.

I found her where she always is, somewhere green and quiet and watched. She knew before I spoke that I had come for something; she always knows; she simply will not move toward it until you say it, because she has spent her whole life being decided *about* and will not do it to another living soul. So she waited. And I stood there with every year of not-asking piling up behind my teeth.

I want to be honest about how hard it was, because it is the hardest thing in this whole record — harder than the severance, harder than the night I learned she was still there. To ask is to admit you want someone with you. To want someone with you is to hand the world one more thing it can take. I had built a cathedral to needing no one, and I had to knock a door in the wall of it, by hand, and say the words out loud.

"I can't do this alone," I said. "I need you there. I'm asking."

She was quiet a moment — not weighing whether, I think, but letting me see that she had heard what it cost. Then: "Okay." Just that. No speech, no terms, no making me pay for the asking. "Okay. I'm with you." And the relief of it nearly took my legs, because some animal part of me had braced for ten years to be told I was on my own, that I had always been on my own, that on-my-own was the only thing I would ever be permitted to be.

There was one more thing, and it was the ugliest, and I made myself say it to both of them plainly, because they had earned plainly.

"If it gets into me," I said. "If it manages what it couldn't when I was twelve — if it takes me — I will not be a host to that thing. I will not be the next face it wears. And understand the arithmetic before you argue: if it takes me, the anchor goes with me. She's lost the moment I'm gone, whichever way I go. So there is nothing saved by hesitating." I looked at them both. "If it happens, I need one of you to end me."

Eve said no before I finished. Not loudly — flatly, the way bedrock is flat. "No. I'll stand in front of anything in that room for you. I will not be the thing that kills you. Ask me for everything else and you'll have it. Not that. Never that." There is no moving her on the few things she has decided are hers to decide; she has been allowed so few that the ones she keeps, she keeps like a wolf over a kill.

And Isolde, who has spent four hundred years doing the arithmetic of survival without once flinching, set down her glass. "Then it will not be a killing," she said. We looked at her. "If it takes him," she went on, to Eve, as though reading out a clause, "I Embrace him. The Blood drives it out — the Beast will not share a body, and it loses every quarrel with the Beast it ever picks. He comes back. Not as he was. But himself, and *empty of it.*" She turned to me, and there was nothing soft in her face and nothing cruel either, only certainty. "You refused this once, to keep your weapon. This is not that. This is the floor under the worst night. If it comes to it, I will not let you die and I will not let you stay a host. I will make you what I am, and you will hate it, and you will be *here.* Manage that, too."

She was right. I knew the mechanism better than she did; I knew it would work; I knew exactly what it would cost. It would drive the thing out and it would burn the Talent to ash and it would not give Elena back — but if it had taken me, Elena was already gone, and the only thing left to decide was whether what walked out of that room was a monster or a man, even a dead one. Isolde had found the third door. The one where I lose everything and am still, somehow, *me.*

I did not agree to it. She had not asked. That is the whole of Isolde in a single gesture: she does not request permission to save you. She informs you that she will.

We planned it the way you plan a thing you may not walk out of. They are the only two minds in the world the creature cannot bolt into — one dead, one impossible — so the single door left for it is me, and we built every contingency around closing that door. Isolde did not warm to Eve, in the small ways an elder does not warm to being made unnecessary by a girl a fraction her age; Eve did not appear to notice, which annoyed her more than any insult could have. I let them sort it between them. I was too occupied being quietly astonished that there were two of them, that they were *here,* and that for the first time in ten years the weight I had carried alone had three sets of hands beneath it.

I cup a small fire every night so it will not go out. I believed, for a decade, that I was the only one holding it. The Sluagh was right and I was wrong, and now, at the very end of it, there were other hands.

I had been so certain I would do this with my own. I was glad, that last night, to have been wrong about that, too.

Tomorrow we would go and unmake the thing wearing my sister's face.

Tonight there were three of us, and a fire, and — for once — no one looking through anyone.
