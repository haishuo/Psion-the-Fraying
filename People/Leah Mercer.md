# Leah Mercer: The Ordinary Witch

![Leah outside the cottage](../images/Marcus%20and%20Leah/Verbena.png)

**"Stop making it weird, Marcus. Come inside. I'll put the kettle on."**

---

## Overview

Leah Mercer is a Verbena witch who has made no effort whatsoever to become mysterious.

She lives with a small Circle on an old farm in Somerset County, Pennsylvania, in the sort of place Marcus would describe as a cottage because every more precise word would sound like he had been paying attention. She grows food and medicinal herbs. She knows which floorboard sticks, which neighbor will trade eggs for salve, and how long the roof has before it needs replacing. She performs rites with blood because blood is alive, intimate, and costly — and then she scrubs the bowl, hangs the cloth to dry, and gets on with the rest of her day.

That is the point of her. Leah is not a remote priestess, an ancient soul in a young body, a prophecy with cheekbones, or a supernatural answer to Marcus's grief. She is aggressively normal: practical, affectionate, stubborn, occasionally impatient, and embedded in a life whose stakes remain real even when they are small. Her magick does not remove her from ordinary life. It roots her more deeply inside it.

Leah is **twenty years old** when she meets twenty-one-year-old Marcus. She belongs to the **Laurel Hill Circle**, a four-witch Verbena household on an old farm in Somerset County, Pennsylvania, in the Laurel Highlands. She is a capable working healer rather than the Circle's leader: **Arete 3; Life 3, Spirit 2, Prime 1, Mind 0.** At first, however, she is simply **Leah** — and that is all Marcus manages to retain despite being a Memory specialist.

---

## Appearance

At twenty, Leah has long dark curls, brown eyes, and the easy physical confidence of someone accustomed to working with her hands. In the garden she dresses for dirt and weather; around the cottage she favors soft, practical clothes that can survive flour, herbs, and smoke. The flowers in her hair and old-fashioned layers are personal taste rather than priestly vestment.

In ritual she can look like the witch outsiders expect: dark green and red, metal charms, herbs at her belt, blood marking her hands and face. The resemblance ends as soon as the rite does. The blood washes off. The jewelry goes back in its box. There is probably still something on the stove.

The contrast is not a disguise. Both versions are Leah.

## Who She Is

### Aggressively Normal

Leah does not confuse solemnity with depth. She can hold a life in her bloody hands and still object when someone tracks mud through the kitchen. She likes tea, dancing, growing things, competent work, and people who say what they mean. She has chores she resents, neighbors who irritate her, and strong opinions about tasks Marcus insists on doing the least efficient possible way.

She is warm without being indiscriminately welcoming. Her first instinct toward a stranger at her door is not mystical recognition but suspicion, especially when that stranger is a young, dour-looking man asking to enter the mind of someone she loves. The Circle is small. Its members protect one another because no institution is coming to do it for them.

Leah's directness is the quality Marcus finds most disarming after her face. She does not play social chess. She notices when he is building a careful verbal structure to avoid saying something simple, lets him finish if she is feeling charitable, and then answers the thing he was trying not to say.

### A Verbena Healer

Leah's practice begins with the body: blood, breath, pain, fever, fertility, food, sleep, soil, season, and the slow work by which damaged living things become capable of living again. Her rites can be visceral. They are never careless. Blood matters precisely because it belongs to someone; using it without regard for consent or cost would empty the rite of its meaning.

She does not understand healing as returning a person to an untouched earlier state. Living things do not move backward. Skin closes around a scar. A bone knits with a seam. Recovery changes the person who recovers, and demanding that they become exactly who they were before is only another way of refusing to see them as they are.

A scar is the body's memory of an injury. The body does not forget what happened when the wound closes; it carries the event forward in changed tissue. What it stops doing is bleeding from it. Leah understands that distinction so completely that she rarely needs to put it into words.

That understanding is why Marcus can work beside her. He cannot *fix* the Circle-sister the Technocracy hurt, and Leah would distrust him if he claimed he could. He can find routes in the mind that terror has buried or broken. He can separate an implanted association from the memory it poisoned, restore access to something that has become unreachable, and help the survivor hold what happened without being trapped perpetually inside it. He cannot make it unhappen.

As Marcus eventually explains it, he does for the mind what Leah does for the body.

Neither of them mistakes that for leaving no scar.

In game terms, Leah has **Arete 3, Life 3, Spirit 2, Prime 1, and Mind 0**. She can diagnose and treat grave bodily harm, work with the spirits of place and season, and supply the small amount of Prime needed for a working rural practice. She is not secretly an archmage. Her competence is practiced, local, and considerable within its proper domain.

The absence of Mind is important. Leah can treat what fear, sleeplessness, starvation, drugs, and prolonged stress have done to Nora's body, but she cannot safely enter the architecture the New World Order built inside Nora's memories. Marcus is not a more powerful version of Leah. He possesses the one form of expertise the house lacks.

### Protective, Not Possessive

Leah is fiercely protective of her Circle-sister, but the injured woman is not Leah's property and not merely the event through which Leah meets Marcus. The sister's consent governs the work. Leah can question Marcus, watch him, and insist that he explain himself; she cannot consent on someone else's behalf.

What changes Leah's opinion is not a grand gesture. It is the accumulation of restraint. Marcus states what he can do and what he cannot. He does not enter where he has not been invited. He does not treat symptoms as inconvenience or the survivor as a puzzle whose successful solution proves his brilliance. When progress stalls, he stays patient. When a memory hurts, he does not call the pain failure.

Leah begins by watching him because she expects to catch him doing harm. Eventually she watches him because she has begun to care whether he remembers to eat.

### Her Own Center

Leah is not waiting at the cottage for Marcus's plot to arrive. The Circle is her family, her responsibility, and the community in which her magick has meaning. Its garden, patients, rites, quarrels, and seasonal obligations continue whether Marcus is present or not. She does not abandon that life to join his hunt because her work is not less important for being local and sustaining rather than singular and terminal.

She also has ordinary rough edges. Her directness can become impatience with people who need time before they can name what they feel. Protectiveness can shade into deciding what is best for someone before remembering to ask. She distrusts institutions for excellent reasons and sometimes extends that distrust to individuals who have not earned it. Leah can recognize those failures and apologize, but being a healer does not make her invariably gentle or right.

Marcus matters to her because she chooses him, not because he becomes the center around which her prior life was secretly arranged. When he leaves, she grieves and continues. When he returns, he is asking to enter a life still in progress.

## The Laurel Hill Circle

To outsiders who require a name, the four Awakened Verbena of the farm are the **Laurel Hill Circle**. Among themselves they usually call the Circle and its home simply **the house**. A few unAwakened relatives, friends, and regular patients live nearby or move through its edges, but the household's magical core is four witches.

Leah is the strongest Life-worker among them, not their leader. An older member holds the deed and handles most outside decisions; another has greater responsibility for the wards and local spirits. Before her capture, **Nora Ellis**, twenty-nine, served as the Circle's principal liaison beyond the Laurel Highlands. Roles overlap in a household this small, and authority depends more on the matter at hand than on a formal hierarchy.

The house is an old stone-and-timber farmhouse on roughly eighteen acres in Somerset County, Pennsylvania. Marcus calls it a cottage because admitting that he noticed the distinction would expose how quickly he began imagining himself there. The property has an orchard, a serious kitchen garden, a spring, several outbuildings, a barn roof that leaks, and a boiler old enough to constitute a recurring antagonist. The spring is also a modest Node: dependable and useful to the people who tend it, but neither powerful nor prestigious enough to make the farm an important Verbena holding.

The Circle pools ordinary incomes. Its members take veterinary work, sell produce and preserves, provide caregiving, and perform repairs and other seasonal labor. They are cash-poor, not playacting at a fantasy commune. The farm can feed and house another person far more easily than it can produce Marcus's usual fee, which is why room, board, and work become a plausible arrangement even before they become his excuse to stay.

## The Job

The canonical first-person telling of this period appears in [Room and Board](../Fiction/10%20-%20Room%20and%20Board.md).

Marcus is twenty-one when he meets twenty-year-old Leah during a genuine lull. The psidolon has gone quiet. His Virtual Adept, Glass Walker, and Nosferatu contacts have nothing. Paying work has dried up with the leads, and grief does not suspend rent.

[Doreen](../Fiction/04%20-%20Strays.md), the Bone Gnawer who taught Marcus how to read the overlooked world and sends him her people's quiet jobs, sends word about a job west of his usual ground. She knows the Laurel Hill household because its witches have quietly treated Bone Gnawers, kinfolk, and other people who cannot safely walk into an ordinary clinic. She knows Marcus because he has honored their standing arrangement without making charity into leverage. She also knows he is broke.

The Circle lost Nora Ellis to the Technocratic Union and got her back alive. The likely captors were the New World Order. Whatever they did, they left injuries the Circle's own healing cannot reach cleanly. Someone who specializes in Memory might.

The witches do not have much money. Marcus would ordinarily decline. He works for cash because landlords do not accept supernatural tragedy as payment, and underfunded rescue work has an ugly way of becoming dangerous charity. But he has no lead, no better client, and no credible plan for making rent. He agrees to look.

Marcus has a speech for first meetings. It establishes that the arrangement is professional, defines the service, places limits on responsibility, names the fee, and ensures that nobody mistakes employment for loyalty. He has given some version of it to vampires, werewolves, mages, frightened parents, and people who were already lying to him. It is polished enough to survive interruption.

Then the cottage door opens and Leah steps outside.

Marcus's mind goes blank.

Not figuratively. For several seconds, the most disciplined Memory specialist of his generation cannot retrieve a practiced introduction he has delivered dozens of times. Leah sees a silent, travel-worn young man staring at her and concludes, reasonably, that the friend's recommendation may have been a mistake.

He recovers enough to give his name. He never gives the speech.

### Nora Ellis

Nora is twenty-nine and was the house's principal connection to the wider supernatural world: the person most likely to carry messages, arrange exchanges, and know which roads and safe places remained usable. That is why the New World Order chose her. Its objective was not merely to interrogate one rural witch but to use her as the weak link through which it could map Tradition safehouses, contacts, and movement across the region.

Nora did not escape. The NWO deliberately released her as a compromised asset. Beneath the obvious trauma are false memories, conditioned associations, reporting compulsions, and triggers meant to turn familiar people and places into an involuntary surveillance network. Some are designed to disclose information; others are meant to steer her back toward handlers without her recognizing the choice as foreign.

Marcus identifies and dismantles that architecture. He does not erase what happened, manufacture an untouched earlier Nora, or promise that every buried mechanism can be removed without consequence. Most importantly, **Nora herself consents to the work**. Leah can protect her, question Marcus, and remain present when Nora asks; she cannot substitute her own permission for Nora's. Nora is the patient and survivor, not the object exchanged between Marcus and Leah's stories.

### The Days After

Leah remains dubious. Marcus is young, closed-off, visibly carrying something he refuses to discuss, and asking for extraordinary trust near a woman the Union has already violated. His manner does him no favors: professional caution sounds cold when heard inside a home organized around mutual care.

Over the following days, he proves himself trustworthy through the work. Leah sees the distinction between secrecy and indifference. Marcus does care; he simply has spent years making sure care cannot be used to find him. He sees in turn that Leah's suspicion is not ignorance of his craft. It is the first obligation of someone guarding an injured sister.

The recovery is incomplete because all recovery is incomplete. The Circle-sister is not restored to a saved copy of herself from before the Union took her. She is able to reach parts of her life that had been sealed behind terror, to recognize some things the NWO put into her as foreign, and to live without every ordinary stimulus reopening the same locked room. What remains is scar, not failure.

The formal work takes a little over two weeks. Marcus then spends roughly another month maintaining that the revised account requires his labor. By the time the Nosferatu lead arrives, he has been at the cottage for about seven weeks — long enough to have routines, unfinished jobs, a particular place at the table, and things in the garden that he planted himself.

When the work is done, Marcus realizes two things. The Circle cannot pay enough to solve his rent problem — and he does not want to leave.

He offers a practical revision to their agreement. The cash is inadequate; room and board have equivalent value; the property needs work; he is capable of doing it. He can repair fences, carry supplies, split wood, work in the garden, and make himself useful until the account is square.

This is transparent nonsense. Leah accepts it without humiliating him by saying so.

## Marcus and Leah

### The Thing He Does Not Name

Marcus tells himself he stays because the revised payment is rational. He may even believe it at first. But this is the first place he has been reluctant to leave since he began the hunt, and Leah is the reason.

The life around her is made of everything his own architecture excludes: meals that happen at a table, work whose value is visible by evening, people who expect him back, affection that does not need to become leverage, tomorrow imagined as something other than another day closer to a kill. Leah does not offer him escape from purpose. She simply makes purpose stop being the only thing in the room.

He falls in love without deciding to and therefore spends a considerable period behaving as though no such event has occurred.

Leah starts sooner and is less confused about it.

### The Message

Then [Cherub](../Fiction/05%20-%20Clear%20Terms.md), his Nosferatu contact, pings him with a hit on the psidolon.

Marcus's first response is irritation.

It lasts only a moment, but the moment matters. For nearly ten years, a lead has been the nearest thing he has to hope. He has bartered, bled, lied by omission, and lived out of cheap rooms for the next one. Now, for the first time, the call arrives and some part of him resents it for taking him away from where he is.

Then the old architecture locks back into place. Elena. The reason. The job he cannot abandon.

Leah does not ask him to stay. She knows that asking would only make the leaving crueler, and she understands enough by then to know that love cannot be built by demanding he become someone else before he is ready. She lets him go because she knows what it is to follow blood back to a wound.

That does not mean she approves of the price he is willing to pay.

### The Return

If Marcus survives the destruction of the psidolon, he eventually returns to the cottage. By then Marcus is twenty-two and Leah is twenty-one: still almost absurdly young, despite everything that has already happened to them.

He has rehearsed explanations on the journey. There may be unfinished work. He may still owe the Circle. He was nearby. He thought he should report the outcome in person. Every explanation becomes less convincing as he says it, because the honest sentence is very small and he has no practice saying it: *I wanted to come back to you.*

Leah lets him struggle for a while. Then she tells him to stop making it weird and come inside. She will put the kettle on.

This is what rescue looks like between them. Not a spell, a revelation, or absolution. A door held open and an ordinary thing happening next.

### The Wound Closes

For most of his life, Marcus does not merely remember Elena. He believes he must preserve her **exactly as she was**: every feature, inflection, joke, and unremarkable childhood detail held in perfect condition through the nightly ritual. Letting one piece soften into ordinary memory would mean failing her. Forgetting the precise shade of her eyes would make him an accomplice to the psidolon and the MetaConcert, completing by neglect the erasure they began by force.

The Euthanatos revelation shakes that belief without freeing him from it. Learning that Elena survives as a wraith and that **Marcus himself is her Fetter** proves that their bond has accomplished something real. The ritual is the most visible expression of that bond, not the machinery sustaining it, but Marcus initially hears no distinction. *I must remember or I fail her* becomes *I must perform this perfectly or I kill her again.* The revelation exposes the architecture of his grief, and his trauma immediately converts it into an even more terrible duty.
Leah never tells Marcus to let Elena go.

She knows that saying it directly would harm him. He would not hear an invitation to heal; he would hear a request to erase his sister. He would brace around the wound, defend the ritual, and retreat into the part of himself that survived by treating pain as duty. Leah does not make his grief a contest she intends to win, does not ask him to shorten the nightly hour, and does not present herself or their future as something he must choose *instead of* Elena.

She simply lives beside him according to an ethic his grief has never permitted him to apply to himself. Wounds are cleaned and closed. Scars are touched without shame. A healed body is never treated as a body to which nothing happened, and continued bleeding is never demanded as proof that the injury mattered. Leah makes room for Elena's name in the cottage. She listens when Marcus speaks of her and does not test the fidelity of his archive. Elena is allowed to be his sister rather than only his obligation.

Leah might, in some unrelated moment over some ordinary injury, observe that the body remembers what hurt it even after it stops bleeding. She would not turn the sentence toward Marcus. She knows better. He is the one who eventually does.

Over time, Marcus understands that perfect preservation and faithful remembrance are not the same act. A living memory is allowed to soften at the edges, change its emotional weight, and sit quietly for days without being summoned and inspected. He can forget some tiny detail and still have loved Elena. He can stop reopening the wound and the scar will remain. Elena does not need to be held at eight years old, exact and endangered, for the rest of his life in order to have existed.

The ritual changes gradually. He shortens it. One night he misses it and discovers that love did not vanish while he slept — and Elena's Fetter did not break. Eventually he stops performing the perfect nightly reconstruction at all. He still remembers Elena; he always will. But he remembers her as a brother remembers his dead sister, not as the sole archivist of a person the world will delete the moment his concentration breaks. Elena is anchored to **Marcus**, not to the flawless performance of his grief.

This is what it means for Marcus to let Elena go. He does not decide she matters less. He stops requiring himself to bleed forever as proof that she mattered at all. Leah does not deliver that conclusion to him. She is simply there when he becomes able to reach it himself.

In the authored future, Elena eventually makes the corresponding choice from her side. Once she knows Marcus survived, returned, and learned how to live, she voluntarily resolves him as her Fetter. That is not Marcus forgetting her and not Elena being dragged into Oblivion; it is a dead sister no longer needing to anchor herself to her brother's wound. Whether she Transcends or passes somewhere Marcus cannot follow remains unknown. The release is hers. The love remains theirs.

## Why They Work

**Mind and body.** Marcus preserves roads through memory; Leah helps living flesh find a way to continue. Neither promises restoration without change. In the beginning, Marcus can apply that ethic to Leah's Circle-sister but not to himself. His final growth is understanding that the mind, like the body, can remember an injury without remaining an open wound.

**Peers, differently formed.** Leah is not older, wiser, or responsible for rehabilitating a younger man. They meet at twenty and twenty-one. Marcus has spent adolescence becoming extraordinarily competent at danger, secrecy, and terminal purpose; Leah has spent hers embedded in a Circle, learning how bodies, relationships, gardens, and households survive over time. Each is mature where the other is least practiced, and conspicuously young whenever circumstance reaches outside that competence.

**Precision and directness.** Marcus constructs careful language because precision has kept him alive. Leah goes through it when precision becomes avoidance. She does not make him less exact; she makes him use exactness to tell the truth.

**Purpose and life.** Leah is not a replacement for Elena and never competes with the dead. She is evidence that loving Elena does not require Marcus to refuse every living person who might love him. She does not end the hunt. She is the first credible answer to *after.*

**Usefulness and belonging.** Marcus knows how to make himself useful anywhere. The harder lesson is that Leah wants him present when there is nothing that needs fixing. Their relationship begins to become real when room and board no longer require an account.

**No mind-reading shortcut.** Marcus's power cannot be allowed to replace intimacy. With Leah, trust depends on asking, listening, and sometimes not knowing. He does not read her to settle an argument, reassure himself, or learn what she is not ready to say. The boundary is not incidental to their relationship. It is one of the reasons she can trust him with it.

## What Leah Is Not

- **Not Marcus's reward.** Surviving does not earn him a woman. A relationship with Leah is a life he must participate in after survival makes participation possible.
- **Not his therapist.** She can love him, challenge him, and sit beside grief. She cannot do his healing for him, and she knows better than most that healing is work performed by the injured living thing. Crucially, she never instructs him to "move on" or "let Elena go." Marcus must arrive there himself.
- **Not secretly cosmic.** Leah need not be an archmage, an oracle, a lost heir, or a singular node in the setting's metaphysics. Her narrative force comes from being one competent Verbena among a small Circle and one ordinary woman who matters enormously to one man.
- **Not harmless.** Normality is not softness. She is a blood-working witch whose Circle has survived the attention of the Technocracy, and she will defend her people. The apron and the ritual knife belong to the same person.
- **Not a cure for Marcus.** Love gives him somewhere to go. It does not erase Elena, restore his parents, undo the MetaConcert, or make ten years disappear. Leah does not heal Marcus; she is present while he heals.

## The Authored Future

In the authored **Memories** telling, the images of Marcus and Leah married, expecting a child, raising a family, and growing old together depict their canonical long future. It is the answer to the question Marcus refuses to ask for most of his life: what if the story does not end when the monster does?

In that future, domesticity is not a retreat from the supernatural world. The cottage remains a Verbena home. Blood rites still happen beyond the garden. Marcus may still perform difficult Memory work, perhaps deprogramming people whom the Cult of the Unbound is trying to turn, and danger does not politely disappear because he has children at breakfast. What changes is the architecture of his life. The work has to bring him home. Elena becomes part of that home too: someone Marcus can remember in stories and ordinary affection, without withdrawing every night to prove that he still can.

The setting toolkit still leaves Marcus's ending available to individual chronicles: a table can stop at tea, take them somewhere harder, or never allow Marcus to return at all. The fiction, however, commits. Its essential fact is not merely marriage or children. It is that Leah makes a future imaginable to him before he knows how to want one, and Marcus ultimately chooses to inhabit it.

## Details Still Open

The core decisions are fixed. Only details that do not change the shape of Leah's story remain open until a scene needs them:

- Leah's exact birthday
- the names and full histories of the Circle's older deed-holder, its principal ward-keeper, and the mundane people around the house
- Nora's precise life and role after the first stage of recovery
- the rites and circumstances by which Elena ultimately resolves her Fetter, and whatever lies beyond Marcus's ability to know

These are attachment points, not undecided foundations.

## Themes

**The ordinary as a future** — Marcus has survived by treating ordinary life as expendable overhead. Leah reveals it as the thing all the terrible work was supposed to protect.

**Healing leaves a scar** — Body and mind share an ethic: recovery is real even when the past remains true. The scar is memory; the absence of bleeding is not forgetting.

**Love without replacement** — Leah does not displace Elena. Loving the living is not betrayal of the dead, and letting a wound close is not erasing the person whose loss made it.

**Home as a verb** — The cottage matters because of repeated acts: planting, cooking, repairing, returning, making tea. Home is not where Marcus stops moving. It is where he chooses to come back.

**Aggressive normality** — The witchcraft is real. So are rent, breakfast, muddy boots, and the roof. None cancels the others.

---

*Marcus Thiel spent ten years believing the story ended when one of them died. Leah is the woman who opens the door after the ending and asks whether he is coming in.*
