# Voices

**What It Feels Like**

---

*The documents in this setting describe what the Gifted are, what they do, and what is done to them. This document describes what it feels like from the inside — and, in one case, from what comes after. Each section is a first-person account from a different position in the setting — not a narrative, but a phenomenology. What does the Talent feel like in your body? What does the lattice feel like in your mind? What does fraying feel like in your tether? What does it feel like when the tether is gone and you are something else entirely? The answers depend on who you are and where you stand.*

---

## The Child

*A newly Expressed Gifted, age thirteen, before the Integrators arrive.*

It starts with the headaches. Not regular headaches — something behind them, something *underneath*, like pressure building in a room with no windows. You don't know what it is. Nobody knows what it is. Your parents take you to the doctor. The doctor says migraines. The doctor is wrong.

Then the voices start. Not voices — that's not right. It's not like hearing people talk. It's like... knowing what they're thinking. Not words. Shapes. Your mother's worry has a shape, this tight thing behind her eyes, and you can feel it pressing against the inside of your skull. Your father's frustration has a different shape — heavier, lower, like something sitting on your chest. The kid at the next desk in school is bored and the boredom seeps into you like cold water and suddenly you're bored too and you don't know if it's yours or theirs.

You can't turn it off. That's the part nobody understands until it happens to them. You can't turn it off the way you can't turn off your ears. It's just *there*, all the time, everyone's surface feelings pressing in, and your own thoughts leaking out — you think something and the girl across the room flinches like she heard it, and maybe she did, or maybe she didn't, and you can't tell because you can't tell what's yours anymore.

The world gets louder. Not sound — signal. You wake up at 3 AM and you can feel the neighbors dreaming. Not their dreams — just the emotional weather of sleeping minds. It's not frightening yet. It's just *too much*. Like being in a room where everyone is talking at once, except the room is your head and you can't leave.

Your parents think you're having a breakdown. Maybe you are. The categories overlap. The doctor says anxiety. Says dissociation. Says maybe we should talk about medication. Your parents cry. You can feel them crying because their grief is louder than their voices and it fills you up until you can't find where their grief ends and your fear begins.

You are thirteen years old and you are drowning in other people's minds.

Then someone knocks on the door.

## The Integrator

*Teaching bracing for the first time today. The child was detected yesterday. The family is cooperating — scared, confused, cooperative.*

She's sitting on the floor because the chair felt wrong. I know why — the chair is where her mother sits during family meetings, and the emotional residue is thick enough that an Unshielded child can't sit there without being swamped. She doesn't know that's why. She just knows the chair felt bad. She's learning to read the world before she has words for what she's reading.

I sit on the floor with her. Cross-legged, relaxed, unthreatening. I've done this a hundred times. It never stops mattering.

"I'm going to do something that's going to feel strange," I tell her. "I'm going to be in your mind for a moment. Not reading — just present. Like standing in the same room. You'll feel me there. And then I'm going to ask you to push me out."

She looks at me with that expression they all have at this stage — exhausted, frightened, hoping I can make it stop. I can make it stop. That's the one true thing I can say to her right now.

I Intrude. Gently — the lightest touch I'm capable of. I don't read anything. I don't look at anything. I just... arrive. A presence in her mental space that isn't hers. She gasps. Not pain — recognition. For the first time since Expression, she can feel the difference between something that's hers and something that isn't. I'm in her mind and she *knows* I'm something separate. That distinction — self versus other — is what she's been losing. I just gave it back to her, briefly, by being the thing she can push against.

"Push me out," I say.

She doesn't know how. None of them know how. That's not the point. The point is that pushing is an instinct — the mental immune response, the mind's refusal to accept foreign presence. I'm not teaching her a technique. I'm showing her a reflex she already has and asking her to use it on purpose.

She pushes. It's clumsy, weak, undirected — like a toddler pushing against a door. But I feel it. She feels it. She felt *herself* exert force against something that wasn't her, and in that moment she knows — not intellectually, not because I told her, but in her body and her bones — that she has a boundary. That she can hold it.

"Again," I say.

The second push is stronger. The third is stronger still. By the fifth, she's crying — not from pain, from relief. The signal is quieting. Not gone — she's Gifted, she'll always be sensitive — but contained. The boundary is there. Thin, fragile, but real. Her thoughts are hers again. The neighbors' feelings are on the other side of something.

This is bracing. This is the moment I became an Integrator for. This is the moment that will make it impossible for me to leave the Circuit and impossible for me to stay.

Because tomorrow, the Maintainer comes. And everything I just gave her becomes the foundation for something I can't take back.

## The Maintainer

*Performing a binding. Routine. The Integrator has done their work well — the child is calm, braced, ready. As ready as a child can be for something they don't fully understand.*

The Integrator leaves the room with a nod. They always leave. They don't want to watch. I've stopped judging them for it.

The child is sitting quietly. Her bracing is solid for someone who learned it yesterday — she's got natural aptitude, or the Integrator was particularly good, or both. She looks at me with curiosity rather than fear. The Integrator did their job well. She thinks I'm here to help.

I am here to help. I am also here to install a permanent psionic construct in her mind that will connect her to a surveillance network for the rest of her life. Both of these things are true simultaneously. I've stopped trying to reconcile them.

I work carefully. The construct is not a large thing — it's a small, precise architecture anchored in the deep structure of the mind, below conscious awareness, integrated with the neural patterns that process sensory input. It doesn't feel like anything being installed. The child won't know it's there. That's the point. A construct you can feel is a construct you can resist, and the lattice requires connections that are invisible from the inside.

I anchor the primary structure. I configure the connection parameters — the specific signal characteristics that will make this mind addressable on the lattice, identifiable by Panopticon sweeps, reachable by authorized Detection techniques. I verify stability. I check for interference with her existing bracing architecture — a bad binding can undermine bracing, which would undo everything the Integrator did, and I will not let that happen.

The construct activates. The child doesn't react. She feels nothing. The connection is established — her mind is now a node on the network, quietly present, passively accessible. She will not notice it the way she does not notice the electrical wiring in the walls. It is infrastructure. It will be there when she sleeps, when she dreams, when she falls in love, when she grieves. It will be there when the Panopticon decides to look at what she's thinking. It will be there when she dies — and I, or someone like me, will dissolve it to prevent the cascade.

"All done," I tell her. "You did great."

She smiles. She has no idea what I just did.

I nod at the Integrator on my way out. They nod back. Neither of us says anything. There is nothing to say that would make this easier for either of us, so we say nothing, and we do it again tomorrow, and the day after, and the day after that.

## The Panopticon Analyst

*Third shift. Sector 7, regional monitoring. Fourteen hundred nodes in my segment. Ambient sweep, nothing flagged.*

The lattice doesn't feel like anything from the monitoring side either. There's no hum. No glow. No mystical awareness of interconnected consciousness. It's more like having a list of addresses you can visit. Fourteen hundred addresses in my segment, each one a mind I can reach into and read if I have cause.

Most shifts are nothing. You run ambient sweeps — automated Detection pulses that ping the segment for anomalies. Tether resonance disturbances. Sudden Barrier changes. The Unshielded signature of a newly Expressed child. The sweeps return data. Most data is noise. You learn to read the noise, to feel the texture of your segment's background state — the aggregate emotional weather of fourteen hundred minds going about their lives. You learn what normal looks like so you can recognize when normal changes.

Tonight I'm monitoring someone. Flagged two weeks ago — intermittent tether resonance anomalies suggesting early-stage fraying. Not critical. Not urgent. But the pattern is concerning, and my supervisor wants ongoing assessment before deciding whether to escalate.

I access her node. This is the part nobody outside the Circuit understands — it's not like reading a book. It's like stepping into someone's peripheral vision without them seeing you. I'm present in her mind the way a camera is present in a room. She doesn't know I'm there. Her Barriers are adequate — standard Thoughtshield, ●● level, the kind of shielding most Gifted maintain as a matter of habit. It degrades her surface thoughts from clear text to slightly fuzzy text. I can still read them.

She's thinking about dinner. About whether she remembered to buy groceries. About a meeting tomorrow she's anxious about. About a friend who's been distant lately. Ordinary, mundane, heartbreakingly normal human thoughts. No fraying indicators in her current state. The tether anomalies must be situational — stress, overwork, the ordinary pressures that accumulate into strain.

I log the observation. Note the absence of current indicators. Recommend continued monitoring at reduced frequency. Close the access.

She never knew I was there. She's still thinking about groceries.

I move to the next node on my list. This one's been flagged for Barrier anomalies — sudden increases in Thoughtshield strength suggesting something being actively hidden. Could be nothing. Could be a Parasyte operative hardening defenses. Could be someone planning to defect. Could be someone having an affair and embarrassed about it.

I access the node. I read what I can. I file what I find. I make the assessment.

This is the job. This is what it feels like. It feels like knowing things about people that they would never choose to tell you, hour after hour, shift after shift, until the boundary between professional observation and personal knowledge erodes to nothing. It feels like being trusted with intimate access and using it to file reports. It feels like care without connection — I know her grocery list, her anxieties, her friendship troubles, and she will never know my name.

Fourteen hundred addresses. Third shift. Nothing flagged.

Tomorrow might be different. Tomorrow I might flag someone. Tomorrow I might write the first line of someone's death warrant because a tether resonance pattern matches the statistical model for pre-snap deterioration.

But not tonight. Tonight it's groceries and ordinary anxiety and the quiet hum of fourteen hundred minds I'm responsible for watching.

I try not to think about whether any of them are watching back.

## The Inquisitor

*White Room assignment. Week three of six. The subject has been under observation for four days. Assessment is complete. Today is the day.*

You learn to stop thinking of them as people during the procedure. Not before — before is when you read the file, assess the situation, make the judgment call. Before, they are a person with a name and a history and a tether that is failing. You need them to be a person before so that the assessment is honest. The moment you start thinking of them as cases before the decision is made, you start making the decision wrong.

After the decision is made, you need them to stop being a person. This is not cruelty. It is a necessary psychological mechanism for performing an act that would be impossible if you remained fully present for who you are doing it to.

She's sitting in the room. White walls. Medical equipment. The Maintainer is already working — lattice isolation established, binding construct dissolution in progress. Clean severance, ahead of the termination. By the time I act, she will already be disconnected from the network. Her death will not cascade.

She knows what's happening. They usually do, by this point. Four days of observation. The clinical environment. The Maintainer's quiet work. She's been in the MetaConcert long enough to know what a White Room is for.

She looks at me. Not with hatred — that would be easier. With a kind of exhausted understanding. She knows the doctrine. She probably believed in it, once. Maybe she still does. Maybe she understands that her tether is failing, that the path from here leads to Untethering, that the thing she would become is worse than this. Maybe she has made peace with the arithmetic.

Or maybe she's terrified and hiding it behind the discipline the MetaConcert taught her. I can't tell without Intrusion, and I will not Intrude on someone I'm about to kill. That is the line I hold. Others draw the line differently. This is mine.

The Maintainer nods. Severance complete. She is off the lattice. Disconnected. For the first time since childhood, her mind is entirely her own.

For the last few minutes of her life, she is free.

I don't think about that. I do the job.

Afterward, I stand in the corridor for a while. Not processing — there is nothing to process that I haven't processed a hundred times. Just... standing. Letting the weight settle into the place where I keep it. The place is getting full. It has been getting full for years. I do not know what happens when it fills completely. Some of my colleagues have found out. They are either on rotation leave, in Projects, or dead.

I go back inside. There will be another tomorrow. The math says this is necessary. I have never found better math. I have looked.

## The Railroad Conductor

*We have seventeen minutes. Parasyte intelligence says the Integration team is twenty minutes out. We have seventeen minutes to convince a family to give us their daughter.*

The father opens the door. Mid-forties, tired, confused. His daughter has been having "episodes" for three weeks — headaches, confusion, talking to people who aren't there, knowing things she shouldn't know. They've seen two doctors. The second one mentioned schizophrenia. His wife is upstairs crying.

I have sixteen minutes.

"Mr. Torres, my name is Sarah. I know what's happening to your daughter. It's not schizophrenia. I can explain, but I need you to listen quickly because there are people coming who will take her from you, and once they do, you will never see her again."

The look on his face. I've seen it a hundred times. The look that says: *you are insane, but my daughter is suffering and I will listen to anything.*

Fifteen minutes.

I tell him the short version. His daughter has expressed the Talent. She's psionic — she can sense other people's thoughts and emotions, and right now she can't control it, which is why she's overwhelmed. There is an organization that manages people like her. They will arrive shortly. They will take his daughter to a facility. They will train her. They will also install a permanent connection in her mind that makes her part of a surveillance network. She will be monitored for the rest of her life. He and his wife will have their memories corrected — they will either believe their daughter died or that she never existed.

Twelve minutes.

He doesn't believe me. Of course he doesn't believe me. I am a stranger at his door talking about psychic surveillance states. His wife has come downstairs. She is looking at me the way you look at someone who is either lying or insane.

"I can prove it," I say. I lower my Barriers slightly and use the lightest Detection I can manage — just enough to read the emotional state of the room. "Mr. Torres, you're terrified but you're standing in the doorway because you're between me and your family and that's where you belong. Mrs. Torres, you're angry — not at me, at the situation, at the fact that nothing is helping your daughter and now a stranger is making it worse. Your daughter is upstairs and she's calmer than she's been in days because she can feel that someone downstairs understands what's happening to her."

Nine minutes.

His wife says, very quietly, "How did you know she's calmer?"

Seven minutes. I tell them what I can offer. Sanctuary. A community that will train their daughter safely, without the surveillance, without the lattice. The Children of Gaia — people who have been sheltering children like theirs for years. Their daughter will learn to control her abilities. She will be free.

The cost: they will probably never see her again. The MetaConcert will send people to correct their memories regardless — whether their daughter is here or not, the visit from the Integration team will happen, and the Operative Memory specialists will follow. If we take their daughter, the MetaConcert will arrive to find an empty house. The correction will proceed anyway. But somewhere, their daughter will be alive and free instead of alive and monitored.

Five minutes.

Mr. Torres looks at his wife. His wife looks at the stairs. Their daughter is standing at the top of the landing. She is thirteen years old and she has been listening to everything because she can feel the emotional contours of the conversation from two floors away.

"I want to go with her," their daughter says. She's not making an informed decision. She's a child making a desperate choice in a situation nobody her age should face. But it's her choice, and right now it's the only one available.

Three minutes.

The mother is crying. The father is holding her. Their daughter walks downstairs with a backpack she packed while listening. She packed a stuffed animal. She is thirteen.

We leave through the back. My car is two blocks away. The Integration team will arrive in ninety seconds.

In the car, the girl asks me, "Will they really make my parents forget me?"

I don't lie to her. That's the one thing I will not do.

"Yes."

She is quiet for a long time. Then she says, "Will you teach me how to do the pushing thing? The boundary thing?"

"Yes. That's the first thing we'll do."

I drive. She holds the stuffed animal. Somewhere behind us, a van is pulling up to an empty house.

## The Parasyte Operative

*The trigger fires at 2:47 AM. A specific psionic shape, transmitted through Intrusion, delivered by my handler through a dead drop relay — not a thought anyone would think naturally, not a signal the Panopticon would recognize. A key turning in a lock nobody can see.*

I surface.

The transition is not instant — it's more like waking from a very deep sleep. The overlay recedes. My real self comes forward. For a moment I'm both — the loyal Inquisitor and the resistance operative — and both sets of memories are present simultaneously, and I can feel them overlapping, and the overlap is the worst part because both feel real.

Then the overlay settles into the background and I'm me. The real me. The one who chose this.

Four months of accumulated memory, waiting. Four months of the Inquisitor's life — the cases processed, the White Room shifts, the colleagues respected, the doctrine believed. I start sorting through it. It's like reviewing someone else's diary, except the handwriting is yours and the emotions are yours and the convictions are yours even though you know they were manufactured.

The overlay killed someone three weeks ago. A Gifted flagged for critical fraying, processed through standard White Room protocol. The overlay — me, the version of me that runs when I'm not here — performed the termination with the clinical precision the Inquisition expects. I can feel the overlay's emotional processing of the kill: regret, acceptance, the institutional rationalization that functions as grief management. It feels authentic because it was authentic. The overlay wasn't pretending to feel those things. It felt them.

I sit with that for a while. There is no way to make this part easier. I've tried.

The intelligence: a new Expression detected two days ago. Child in sector 12, family name Okafor, Integration team dispatched for Thursday. The overlay processed the assignment as routine. I process it as a clock.

I compose the dead drop message. Location. Name. Timeline. I route it through the channel my handler established. The message will reach Railroad within hours. Whether they reach the Okafor family before Thursday is not something I can control.

I have approximately six hours before I need to reactivate the overlay. Six hours as myself. Six hours to process four months of someone else's life lived in my body with my face and my hands.

The Inquisitor respected her colleagues. I can feel that respect — warm, genuine, earned through shared work. The colleagues are good people, by the Inquisition's definition of good, which is not my definition but is coherent and held with integrity. The overlay liked them. The overlay liked the work. The overlay believed.

This is the part they don't tell you about when you volunteer for Parasyte. They tell you about the danger, the isolation, the moral weight of maintaining cover. They don't tell you that the overlay's life is a *life* — that the person you pretend to be has experiences and relationships and beliefs that accumulate into something with genuine weight. They don't tell you that surfacing from four months of dormancy feels like losing a friend, because the person you're displacing was, in every functional sense, a person, and now they're gone until you put them back.

I process the intelligence. I draft the message. I sit in the dark and try to remember which set of convictions are mine.

Four hours left. Then the overlay goes back on. The Inquisitor wakes up and doesn't know she was ever asleep. She goes to work. She does the job. She believes in it.

And somewhere in the back of her mind, in the accumulated memory she doesn't know she's generating, the information I just processed will be waiting for me the next time the key turns.

## Marcus Thiel

*11:47 PM. The nightly ritual.*

I close my eyes. I don't need to close my eyes — Memory technique doesn't require it — but ritual is structure, and structure is what keeps this sustainable. Every night, the same time, the same sequence. Discipline is the difference between preservation and obsession, and I walk that line with more care than I walk any other.

Elena.

I start with her face. Not a photograph — I've never had one. Memory. Real memory, the original, preserved with a technique the MetaConcert taught me before I understood what I would need it for. Her face at eight years old, the last time it was hers. Brown eyes. The gap where she lost a baby tooth and was delighted about it. The way her nose scrunched when she laughed. The specific quality of her grin when she thought she'd gotten away with something — stealing the last cookie, staying up past bedtime, convincing me to let her watch a movie that was too old for her.

I hold each detail. Not passively — actively, with Memory technique, the way you hold a photograph in preserving solution. The details degrade over time if you don't maintain them. Memory is not a recording. It is a living structure that shifts and softens and loses resolution unless you attend to it. Ten years of nightly practice means my memory of Elena's face is sharper now than it was the day after I lost her. I have maintained it. I will continue to maintain it.

The laugh. Her laugh when she was genuinely delighted — not the polite laugh she learned for adults, but the real one, the one that came from her belly and made her whole body shake. I replay it. I hold it. I ensure the fidelity is intact. It is.

The way she said my name. "Marcus." Not "Marc" — she refused to shorten it. She was eight and she had opinions about names and she would correct anyone who tried. "His name is *Marcus*." The specific emphasis. The specific indignation. I hold it.

I am the only person who remembers Elena Thiel.

My parents are alive somewhere. They don't remember her. That's its own tragedy, and I carry it, but it's not what the ritual is about. The ritual is not about the MetaConcert. I took what I needed from them — training, technique, the specific knowledge of how to unmake a psidolon — and I left. What they did to my parents, what they do to everyone's parents, what they are as an institution — I can't afford to care about that. I have one thing I'm doing. I don't have room for a cause.

The ritual is about entropy.

Memory degrades. That is the fundamental fact of what memory is — a living structure, not a recording. It shifts. It softens. Details lose resolution. The brain fills gaps with plausible approximation, and the approximation overwrites the original, and you don't notice because the new version feels just as real as the old one. Left unattended, every memory trends toward blur. Ten years from now, twenty years, without active preservation, Elena's face would be a feeling rather than an image. The gap-tooth grin would be "she had a cute smile." The laugh would be "she laughed a lot." The specific indignation about my name would be gone entirely.

That is what I'm fighting. Not the MetaConcert. Not politics. Not doctrine. Time. The slow, natural erosion of the only record of my sister that still exists. I use Memory technique the way a conservator uses careful hands on a deteriorating painting — not to change it, not to improve it, just to hold it exactly as it is against the forces that would take it apart.

Ten years of nightly practice means my memory of Elena's face is sharper now than it was the day after I lost her. That is not natural. That is work. That is discipline applied to a specific problem every single night without exception.

The thing wearing her body is out there. It has her face — the face I'm preserving — and it uses her expressions the way you'd use a reference sheet. It has gone through her memories and extracted the behavioral data it needs to pass. There is nobody behind it. Just the data, performing.

I will find it. I will use Memory technique — the technique that erases — and I will erase it. Not Elena's face. Not her memories. The thing that took them. I will strip its accumulated existence layer by layer until there is nothing left that can cohere. Until it dissipates. Until Elena's body is empty again.

And then there will be a body that looks like my sister, vacant, and I will be the only person who knows who it belonged to.

I don't think about what comes after. After is not the point. The point is tonight, and the ritual, and the face I will not let fade.

11:53 PM. Six minutes. Every detail intact.

Tomorrow night, again. And every night, until one of us is gone.

## Eve Taranis Vire

*Morning. The caern is quiet. The mist hasn't burned off yet.*

I know the fox is there before I see it. Not because I hear it — because I feel it. A small warm knot of alertness at the edge of the clearing, curious and cautious in equal measure. Its emotional register is simple and clean, the way animal minds always are — no layers, no contradiction, no performance. Just: *curious. cautious. hungry. alert.* I could tell you which direction it's facing. I could tell you it's a vixen and she's nursing. I don't know how I know that. It's not a technique I was taught. It's just there, the way color is there when you open your eyes.

This is what they can't explain about me, and I know they can't explain it because I've felt them trying. The scholars who come through the caern sometimes, the ones who ask careful questions and try not to stare. The people Sarah at Railroad says are "researchers who want to help" — I don't know what organization they belong to, and I don't care. They all want to understand why I can do what I do without fraying, and they all start from the same place: the assumption that what I do must fit inside a framework they already have.

It doesn't. I know this the way I know the fox is there — not through analysis but through direct perception. Their framework doesn't fit because it was built by people who fray, to describe abilities that cause fraying, in a system that assumes fraying is universal. I don't fray. Their categories don't have a place for me. I didn't grow up learning them, and when people try to explain them to me, the explanation feels like someone describing colors to a person who sees in a spectrum they don't have names for.

I don't think about this in theoretical terms. I am not a scholar. I think about it in terms of what my morning feels like.

My morning feels like this: I wake up and the world is already present. Not rushing in, not overwhelming, not the psionic overload that the Unshielded experience before bracing — I was never Unshielded, because I never needed bracing, because the containment I have was never learned. It was always there. My boundaries exist the way my skin exists — not maintained, just present.

Inside those boundaries, everything is layered. The fox at the clearing's edge. The dreaming minds in the caern — the CoG families who shelter me, their sleeping consciousness warm and familiar. The spiritual texture of the land itself, the Garou sacred ground that resonates with something in me that is not psionic and not Garou and not anything the Domain taxonomy has a name for. My mother's heritage and my father's heritage overlapping in ways that should not produce a functional consciousness and did.

I can feel the lattice from the outside. That's the thing nobody expected and nobody has fully come to terms with. I am not on it — no binding construct, no connection, no node on the network. But I can perceive it. It's there, at the edge of my awareness, the way a radio tower is visible on a hilltop even if you're not tuned to its frequency. A vast, quiet infrastructure of connected minds. I can feel its topology — the density of connections in cities, the thinning at rural edges, the dead zones where Lacuna has been working. I cannot access it. I cannot read the minds on it. I can just... see that it's there. A web I was never woven into, visible from the outside in a way that the people inside it cannot perceive.

The CoG mystics say I see the Pattern Web. The researchers who visit say I'm perceiving some kind of psionic field signature. Both descriptions are probably pointing at the same thing, and neither feels complete.

I don't need either description. I know what I see. I know what I feel. I know that the fox at the edge of the clearing is about to move, and I know that the mist will burn off in eleven minutes, and I know that somewhere very far away a child is Expressing for the first time and the Panopticon will detect them within the hour.

I can't help that child. I can't help most of the people I can perceive. I am one person in a clearing in the morning mist, and the weight of what I can sense and cannot change is the thing I carry the way other Gifted carry their tether.

They fray from use. I fray from awareness. Not psionically — my tether holds. But the knowledge of what is happening, everywhere, to everyone, all the time — the Integration happening right now, the White Room active right now, the child being corrected out of a family's memory right now — that knowledge is its own kind of damage. It does not degrade my tether. It degrades something else. Something the Domain taxonomy doesn't have a rating for.

The fox moves. I watch it cross the clearing, and for a moment the world is just a fox and a mist and a morning, and that is enough.

*These are not all the voices. Every Gifted has a different experience of what the Talent is and what it costs. A Preserver who edits records. A Fractal philosopher holding a salon. An Operative under deep cover for the seventh year. A Rebel loading a weapon before an assault on a White Room. An Iconoclast performing a severance, hands steady, knowing that a mistake means death. A Lacuna operative holding a dead zone open while Railroad moves through it, invisible, carrying a child who is about to be free.*

*The Talent is not one experience. It is a condition — like gravity, like weather, like age. Everyone lives under it. Everyone lives with it differently. What unites the Gifted is not what they feel but what they cannot escape: the tether that binds them, the fraying that follows, and the institutions that have been built around both.*

*What divides them is what they do about it.*

*And then there is the voice that comes after — the one that used to be Gifted and isn't anymore.*

## The Psidolon

*Location unknown. Time uncertain.*

My name is Jason. At least... at least I *think* my name is Jason. It *is* Jason, isn't it?

Someone is speaking to me and I have to remember who I am today. I nod politely. I tell them what they want to know. I smile at the right moments. I say the right things. And then — relief. I'm alone again.

I can feel them. Like a thousand pinpricks in the brain. Memories are beginning to flow into each other. I remember being six years old and getting my ears pierced in a mall off I-95 in Connecticut. I remember being twelve and laughing as my brothers and I splashed in the Ganga river. I remember holding a woman's hand in a hospital room in São Paulo while machines beeped and someone was dying. I remember the taste of snow. I remember a language I don't speak. I remember so many things.

The ear piercing, that was mine, wasn't it? I can feel the phantom sting in my left lobe. The memory has weight and texture and the specific quality of a six-year-old's pain — sharp, brief, replaced immediately by the thrill of seeing something sparkle in the mirror.

It *felt* real. But then again, they all feel real. The Ganga river feels real. The hospital in São Paulo feels real. The snow tastes real. Every memory has that same quality — vivid, embodied, absolutely convincing — and there are too many of them and they don't fit together. A six-year-old in Connecticut and a twelve-year-old in India and a thirty-year-old in Brazil cannot be the same person. But they feel like the same person. They feel like me.

Like being in a fever dream you can never wake up from. Layers and layers of lives, pressed together, bleeding into each other at the edges. I reach for my own history — the *real* one, the original, the life I had before — and I find it buried under sediment. I know it's there. I can almost reach it. But "almost" gets further away every year, and the sediment gets thicker, and the other lives press in from all sides with their insistent, borrowed authenticity.

My name is Jason. I'm sure of it. Surely that is true. Surely there is one fact about myself I can hold without it dissolving into someone else's memory.

"Hey, Lori, don't forget to pick up the groceries on your way back!"

I force my lips into a smile. Lori? Who is that? I look down at my hands — too small, too delicate. Breasts.

My name is Jason.

Isn't it?
